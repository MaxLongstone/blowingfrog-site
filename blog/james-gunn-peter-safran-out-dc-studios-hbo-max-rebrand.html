James Gunn Got Axed From DC, and the Autopsy Report Is Sitting on My Television Under Its Fifth Fake Name

November 10, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

James Gunn Got Axed From DC, and the Autopsy Report Is Sitting on My Television Under Its Fifth Fake Name

Let me tell you about a baby that has been baptized five times.

I started paying for HBO streaming somewhere in the middle of the Game of Thrones fever, around the time I bought a bottle of Johnnie Walker with the Night King frosted on the label, a whisky that tasted like it had been distilled by the undead and bottled by a marketing department. Since then I have paid the same people, every month, for the same Westeros dragons and the same mobsters in tracksuits. The money never changed. The name on the door changed constantly.

It was HBO Go. Then HBO Now, which nobody remembers, including possibly HBO. Then HBO Max. Then the suits decided “HBO” was dead weight, the most respected three letters in television since NBC stopped mattering, and hacked it off like a gangrenous toe. It became Max. Just Max. Like a golden retriever. Like a guy at a bar who won’t tell you his last name.

And then, two years later, in a move I can only describe as a hostage releasing his own ransom note, they put the HBO back. It’s HBO Max again. The current name is the name from two names ago.

You’re thinking, Luis, this is a post about superheroes, why am I reading about apps? Because you’re a dullard who skims, and because I’m about to make it worth your while. Stay. Sit. Good.

STOP.

DC Fact On November 9, 2026, DC Studios announced that co-CEOs James Gunn and Peter Safran are stepping down, effective December 1. Their replacement is Jeremy Latcham, a former Marvel Studios production executive, named president and chief creative officer by Skydance, the company formed when David Ellison’s Paramount Skydance swallowed Warner Bros. Discovery.

The App Is the Patient

Here’s my whole theory, and it fits on a remote control.

The HBO app is the DC Universe in miniature. Same corporate bloodline, same disease. A thing gets built. It wobbles. Instead of tightening a screw, somebody in a corner office smells failure, panics, and calls in the demolition crew. New name. New logo. New press release about “a bold new era.” Repeat until the audience’s brain pan is mush.

And the next baptism is already scheduled. Skydance has said an HBO Max and Paramount+ bundle arrives in early 2027, and the long-term plan is to fuse the two apps into one. So if you’re keeping score at home, baby number six is in the oven. I’d bet a kidney the new name has the word “plus” in it, or no vowels, or a lowercase letter where God never intended one.

Now hold that app in one hand. Pick up the DC Universe in the other. Weigh them.

Same heft.

A Brief, Cursed History of Throwing It All Away

Let’s walk the DC timeline, briskly, like a coroner who’s late for lunch.

Zack Snyder builds a gloomy, slow-motion, rain-soaked cathedral of a universe starting with Man of Steel in 2013. People argue about it. Fine. That’s what people do. Batman v Superman stumbles. Instead of course-correcting, the studio panics mid-production on Justice League, brings in Joss Whedon, and stitches together a Frankenstein’s monster that pleased exactly nobody, by way of a reshoot schedule, approved by a mustache that had to be digitally erased.

Then, the circle of life: the fans scream, and in 2021 the studio releases the Snyder Cut… on the HBO app. Of course it was on the app. The two patients share a hospital bed.

Then 2022. A Batgirl movie, in post-production, roughly $90 million spent, gets shelved for a tax write-off. Not released badly. Not dumped on streaming. Buried in the backyard like a hamster.

That October, Henry Cavill pops up in a Black Adam cameo and announces he’s back as Superman. The studio told him to announce it, by his own account, before it had even hired the people who’d decide whether he was back. Then Gunn and Safran arrive, with a ten-year plan, a slate called Gods and Monsters, and a promise: no greenlight without a finished script. In December, Cavill posts that he will, after all, not be returning. The studio un-continued its own continuity before the new bosses had finished unpacking their desks.

Then Superman in 2025 does real business. Peacemaker has fans. Things look, for one fragile second, like a plan.

Then Supergirl lands this summer and bombs, roughly $126 million worldwide against a reported $170 million budget. Clayface, the R-rated body horror swing, does modest numbers.

And here we are. Four years. New boss.

You see the pattern? It’s not about any one movie. Every regime gets judged on its worst quarter, and every bad quarter triggers the wrecking ball. The studio treats a stumble like a terminal diagnosis and pulls the plug on a patient with a sprained ankle.

STOP.

Pull the car over. Engine off. Hazards on.

Because I need you to look at the other corner of the ring, and I need you to look closely, because the other corner made garbage too. Lots of it.

The Other Corner Keeps Its Garbage

Marvel Studios is not a temple of perfection. Let me list the sins, lovingly.

Iron Man 2. A movie so busy setting up The Avengers it forgot to have a plot. Thor: The Dark World, a film with a villain so forgettable his own mother would need a name tag. Thor: Love and Thunder, which had screaming goats and somehow still felt tired. Secret Invasion, a TV show about shape-shifting aliens infiltrating Earth that was mostly Samuel L. Jackson looking disappointed in a parking garage.

They know it. They F-ing know it. And God bless them for it.

Because here’s the trick, and it’s a magician’s trick, the oldest one: they didn’t bury the bodies. They used them.

Avengers: Endgame, the most expensive victory lap in film history, takes a time-travel heist and sends Thor and Rocket back into… The Dark World. The worst Thor movie. On purpose. And out of that rubble they pull a scene where a broken, beer-bellied Thor gets one last talk with his mother. The weakest film in the franchise became the setting for one of its most human moments.

That’s kintsugi. The Japanese art of fixing broken pottery with gold, so the crack becomes the beautiful part. Marvel took its cracked bowl and painted the crack gold and sold you tickets to look at the crack.

Same with Iron Man 2’s Justin Hammer, Sam Rockwell’s sweaty, dancing knockoff Tony Stark. A minor villain in a weak sequel. Marvel didn’t forget him. They brought him back in a One-Shot short, All Hail the King, rotting in prison, still talking. A loose thread from a bad movie, picked up and tied into a bow years later because somebody in that building remembered.

Remembering is the whole game.

A shared universe is just a promise that the past counts.

Every time DC rebooted, it told its audience, in a voice as clear as a car alarm: nothing you watched here mattered. Why would anybody show up for chapter one of a story whose publisher has a documented habit of setting fire to chapter three?

Gunn’s Statement In his Instagram post, Gunn called the executive job “trying, to say the least” and said he disliked its corporate side. He and Safran stay on as producers on Man of Tomorrow (summer 2027) and The Batman: Part II (2028), and he says he’s writing one more DC project.

The Irony Is Wearing Marvel’s Name Tag

Now the punchline, and it’s a beauty.

Who did Skydance hire to fix the studio that can’t stop rebooting? Jeremy Latcham. A Marvel lifer. One of the guys who was in the building when Marvel learned to live with its *Iron Man 2*s.

So the cure for DC’s panic reflex is… to fire the people in charge, again, and import a man whose entire professional value is that he worked at the company that didn’t fire everybody when things went sideways.

That’s like a guy with a gambling problem curing it by betting the house on a therapist.

I’m not freaking Kevin Feige. I don’t know what goes on in those meetings, and I can’t tell you what Latcham will do. Maybe he’s exactly the right adult. But the hire itself is the tell. You don’t import patience. You stop executing it.

Okay, Fine, Let Me Argue Against Myself

Here’s the strongest case the other side can make, and it’s not stupid.

Marvel isn’t the saint in this story anymore. Since Endgame, the MCU has had its own wobbles, including building a multiverse saga around a villain and then rerouting the whole plan after it lost the actor. Patience has limits when the box office dips for years.

Gunn wasn’t a victim of a hair-trigger. He got four years and a full slate. Supergirl lost money. The owner changed. New owners clean house; that’s not a DC disease, that’s capitalism with a lanyard. Plenty of reporting suggests this was a push, but the official line is that they stepped down, and Gunn himself sounded relieved.

And the HBO renames had reasons. Mergers, a bid to look less “prestige-only” to a broader audience, then a walk-back when that didn’t work.

All true. And that’s my point, chewed and swallowed: there’s always a reason. Every demolition comes with a memo. The memo is never the problem. The problem is reaching for the memo first and the screwdriver never. Marvel, at its best, reached for the screwdriver. Its bad chapters stayed on the shelf and got quoted later. DC keeps reaching for the dynamite, and wonders why the shelf is always empty.

The Baby Is Still in the Font

So here’s where we land.

Somewhere in a server farm, the next name for my HBO app is being focus-grouped by twelve people who’ve never watched The Sopranos. Somewhere in Burbank, a new president is picking out his office chair, and the old ten-year plan is in a cardboard box next to a cactus and a Superman mug.

Two Hollywood superpowers. Both had flops. One kept the receipts and turned them into Thor’s last talk with his mom. The other keeps renaming the baby and wondering why it won’t answer to anything.

I’ll keep paying, by the way. I’m weak, the dragons are good, and somewhere in my cupboard the Night King is still staring at me from an empty bottle, the only thing in this whole saga that never changed its name. Next time I’ll just call the app Gary.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Why are James Gunn and Peter Safran leaving DC Studios?

DC Studios announced on November 9, 2026, that they are stepping down as co-CEOs, effective December 1, to return to full-time creative work. The move follows Skydance’s takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery and a mixed run that included a hit Superman and a box office loss on Supergirl. Some coverage reads it as a push rather than a choice.

Who is replacing James Gunn at DC Studios?

Jeremy Latcham, a former Marvel Studios production executive, was named president and chief creative officer of DC Studios by Skydance.

Is James Gunn still making DC movies?

Yes. He and Safran remain on Man of Tomorrow, due summer 2027, and as producers on The Batman: Part II, set for 2028. Gunn has said he’s writing another DC project.

What happens to the DCU now?

Nothing official yet. Gunn and Safran’s long-term plan is now in the new president’s hands, and no reboot has been announced.

How many times has HBO’s streaming service been renamed?

It launched as HBO Go, added HBO Now as a standalone, became HBO Max in 2020, shortened to Max in 2023, and returned to HBO Max in 2025. Skydance plans to bundle it with Paramount+ in early 2027 and eventually merge the two.

How did Marvel handle its weaker movies?

It kept them in continuity and built on them. Avengers: Endgame sends its heroes back into the events of Thor: The Dark World, and Iron Man 2’s Justin Hammer returned in the short All Hail the King.
