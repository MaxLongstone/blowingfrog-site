Ueno Bank Collapse: Paraguay, Peña and the Fintech Lie

November 8, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

The Bank Lived in Your Phone, the President Lived in the Bank, and the Robot Was Staring at Your Groceries

I’m going to start in Buenos Aires, because that’s where I’m sitting, and because if you want to understand what just happened in Paraguay you need to understand the smell of a country that has been robbed by its banks so many times it keeps a spare key under the mat for them.

Argentines have a word for the moment the bank locks the door with your money inside. Corralito. Little corral. Like you’re livestock. Which, to be fair, is how the bank sees you, only livestock gets fed.

So when the fintechs arrived, this place went feral for them.

The Dinosaurs, the Wallets, and the Great Deregulated Fiesta

Picture the old Argentine bank. Marble floor. A guard with the warmth of Tommy Lee Jones in a parole hearing. A line that moves at the speed of continental drift. A form, in triplicate, signed in front of a notary who appears to be legally dead.

Now picture the pitch: forget all that. Open an account on your phone in five minutes. No branch. No line. No notary corpse. Cash-back. Instant loans. A little pastel app with a cute name that talks to you like a camp counselor.

Of course we took it. We took it like a man three days in the desert takes a glass of water from a stranger without asking what’s floating in it.

And for a while it was glorious. Digital wallets everywhere. QR codes on every empanada stand. Bank branches closing by the hundreds while fintech payrolls swelled. Freedom from the dinosaurs.

Then the bill came. It always comes.

Wallet Fact In March 2026, 10.8% of everything Argentina’s digital wallets and fintech lenders had lent out sat in the “unrecoverable” category. A year earlier that figure was 2.6%. It quadrupled in twelve months. Counting everything behind on payments, about one in every four pesos lent by fintechs and wallets was in some kind of trouble. (Source: iProUP, May 2026.)

One in four pesos. That’s not a loan book. That’s a roulette wheel with a customer service chatbot.

The Central Bank, meanwhile, started tightening the screws in a way the industry reads as a plan to leave only a handful of wallets standing. Some players folded. Some got swallowed by bigger fish. The survivors are walking around with loan books that limp. Here are some examples of what’s being dubbed the “anti-unicorns”: ITTI, Efectivo Sí, Ualá, Lemon Cash, Etermax, Ank… Some of them went belly up, some are barely holding on, and some, and I know a few, are praying to the gods for a bit of respite. But all of them feel a shiver of recognition when, late at night, Ghostbusters comes on and Dr. Peter Venkman swaggers into the Mayor’s office: “Human sacrifice, dogs and cats living together… MASS HYSTERIA!”

The lesson Argentina has been learning, slowly, with the enthusiasm of a toddler learning about stoves, is that a lot of these shiny institutions were never standing on their own legs. They were propped up. Cheap money, promo budgets, and the warm ambient faith that “tech” means “smarter.”

Hold that thought. Grab a mate. We’re driving north.

Meanwhile, Across the River, a Bank Ate a Country

Paraguay. Seven million people. A country foreign investors had been treating like the quiet kid in class who always does the homework. Steady growth, stable economy, the sensible cousin at the Mercosur barbecue.

And in Paraguay there was Ueno.

Ueno Bank went from an obscure finance company to a household name in less than five years. It got its banking license in 2023 and then, as far as I can tell from the outside, ate everything. More than 70 locations. Cash-back on groceries. Sponsor of the national soccer team. Its name plastered on the country’s main stadium, which is the corporate equivalent of getting your face tattooed on the town’s water tower.

By the end, Ueno claimed more than 60% of Paraguay’s adults as customers.

Read that twice. Sixty percent. That’s not a bank, that’s a climate.

Ueno Fact As of October 2026, Ueno reported holding roughly $800 million in public deposits, about a quarter of its total. That included money from the state pension and health fund and from the operators of Paraguay’s giant hydroelectric dams. (Source: AP.)

The dams. The bank had the dam money. If you’d pitched this as a Bond plot, a producer would’ve told you it was too on the nose.

The Week the App Went Dark

Here’s the timeline, and I’m going to keep it clean because the facts don’t need my help. They’re doing fine being horrifying on their own.

Monday, November 2. Central bank regulators examining Ueno’s loans to companies linked to its own owners find poorly documented lending, plus expenses the bank had been counting as assets. Once the books get corrected, Ueno’s capital, the cushion a bank must keep to absorb losses, drops to less than half the legal minimum.

Tuesday. The central bank seizes Ueno. The app goes dark. Cards stop working. Transfers die. Branches lock their doors. Lines form outside with nowhere to go.

Wednesday. Panic spreads to every other bank’s app as people try to open accounts elsewhere, and those apps crash too. Thousands hit the streets of Asunción. Retirees, students, workers. Riot police answer with pepper spray and rubber pellets. The interim central bank president, appointed that very day after his predecessor quit at the president’s request, goes on record with a sentence that deserves to be framed in every finance ministry on earth:

“The banking system is sound.”

Sure, pal. And the Titanic had a lovely string section.

Thursday, November 5. President Santiago Peña announces his resignation in a video posted to X, while lawmakers are literally mid-debate on impeaching him. The lower house votes 78 to 0 to impeach him anyway. His own Colorado Party, which has run the country for most of the last seven decades, votes against him. Congress refuses to accept the resignation. It wants the trial.

This week. The Senate votes to impeach. Vice President Pedro Alliana is sworn in. Prosecutors open three criminal cases. Peña is placed under house arrest, according to CNN, over the bank he once held a stake in. He sold that stake last year. He denies betraying anyone and apologized to the public “if” he failed them at any point, which is the “sorry you feel that way” of national addresses.

Eight senior Ueno executives have been detained, including founder Miguel Vázquez and bank president Juan Manuel Gustale. Twenty-seven officials and associates have been charged with offenses including breach of trust and attempted fraud. Seven thousand Ueno employees are standing outside the Labor Ministry asking for wages that are, you guessed it, stuck inside Ueno.

Nobody has been convicted of anything yet, and I’m not a prosecutor. I’m barely a guy who can balance a checkbook. Don’t ask me the finer points of Paraguayan constitutional procedure, I’m not freaking John Grisham. But the shape of the thing is visible from orbit.

Back in Buenos Aires, the Archive Wakes Up

Over here, the receipts came out before lunch.

Javier Milei had spent two years holding Peña up as the region’s honor student. In April 2025, in Asunción, he said what bound the two of them was a shared love for the ideas of freedom. On camera he called Paraguay an example of what to do economically, for Argentina and for the rest of South America. In December 2025, when S&P bumped Paraguay to investment grade, he congratulated his “dear friend” on X.

The day Peña resigned, the Argentine opposition dug up every one of those clips and posted them like wanted posters.

Fair is fair: praising a neighbor’s growth numbers is not the same as vouching for a bank, and nothing so far ties Milei to Ueno. But it’s a lesson in what the region’s political class was looking at through its telescope. The investment grade. The inflation chart. Never the loan book.

How Do You Get That Big That Fast?

The question every Paraguayan is asking, and it’s a good one: how does a bank linked to the president grow to swallow most of the country while people inside the system are waving red flags?

Exhibit A. Peña appointed Carlos Carvallo, the former president of Financiera Ueno, the finance company Ueno grew out of, to run the central bank. The central bank. The agency that supervises Ueno. That’s not a fox guarding the henhouse. That’s the fox’s former boss running the Department of Henhouses, and the fox has a parking spot.

Exhibit B. In June, central bank inspectors found a pattern of risky lending to insiders, as reported by ABC Color, Paraguay’s big newspaper. Ueno had been lending large sums to companies tied to its owners, beyond what the law allows, without enough collateral to cover the debts. On one single day in September 2025, nearly $39 million went out to three such companies.

Exhibit C. Peña brushed it off. As recently as October he was defending Ueno and calling the worries a smear campaign.

Thirty-nine million dollars. One day. Three companies. Meanwhile somebody’s grandmother in Luque was getting declined for a $200 overdraft because she bought too many eggs.

Which brings me to the robot.

The Robot Was Staring at the Wrong Guy

STOP.

Pull the car over. I need you to see this part with your own eyes.

Ueno was not just a digital bank. It was a digital bank that sold itself on artificial intelligence. Its own 2022 annual report brags about investing in AI, data analytics, neural networks and robotization. And in 2023 it introduced a little AI assistant, with a cute name, of course, whose job, in the bank’s own words, was to study every single movement customers made inside the app so it could decide whether to unlock a credit card or a loan for them.

Every purchase. Every QR payment. Every transfer to your cousin. Every 2 a.m. empanada. The machine watched it all, like a Victorian landlady with a telescope and nothing else to live for.

The machine knew exactly how many times you bought bread this month.

The machine did not, apparently, notice $39 million walking out the side door in one afternoon toward the people who owned the building.

And that, ladies and gentlemen and whatever is running Facebook, is the whole fintech-AI con in one image. The algorithm is a microscope bolted to the floor, pointing down. It scrutinizes the little guy to the third decimal place. It can tell you, with machine-learning confidence, that a retired schoolteacher is a credit risk because she skipped her phone bill in 2019. It has no lens, no angle, no interest whatsoever in looking up at the insiders. Nobody trained it to. Nobody wanted it to. Pointing the microscope up isn’t a technical problem. It’s a decision, and the people who would have to make it are the people it would be pointed at.

Greed doesn’t hate surveillance. Greed loves surveillance. It just wants to be the one holding the telescope.

You thought this was a post about Paraguayan politics. Surprise. It’s a post about who the machine works for. I swerved across three lanes and parked in front of your phone. Look at it. Look at the little smiley app. It’s looking at you too, and it’s taking notes, and it is not taking notes on its own management.

And here’s the cosmic part, the part that makes me want to pour something brown. These systems were sold as the cure for exactly this. The old corrupt banker with the cigar and the cousin on the regulatory board, gone, replaced by clean impartial code. Instead we got the same banker, same cigar, same cousin, now with a neural network guarding the front door so nobody looks at the back one. We didn’t kill the dinosaur. We gave it an app and a cartoon mascot and let it eat the pension fund through a QR code. Somewhere an Elder God of compound interest is reading the quarterly report and purring.

The Fair Part, Because Somebody in the Comments Will Say It

Let me argue against myself before you do it for me.

Fintech is not a scam by nature. Ueno itself claimed it brought hundreds of thousands of Paraguayans into the banking system for the first time, people who had never had an account, never had a card, never been allowed through the marble door. That’s real. Financial inclusion matters, and for plenty of people a phone wallet is the first time a financial institution treated them like a customer instead of a suspect.

The AI didn’t cause this either. By every account so far, Ueno didn’t fall because a credit model went rogue. It fell, allegedly, because humans lent money to themselves and other humans looked the other way. That’s the oldest crime in banking, older than the abacus. The algorithm is an accessory, not the getaway driver.

And Argentina’s wallet problem isn’t Paraguay’s. Argentine fintechs mostly don’t take deposits the way Ueno did. Their mess is a credit hangover, sky-high rates meeting a population borrowing to buy groceries. Different disease. Same fever.

All true. And none of it changes the core point: a technology that’s capable of watching everything was aimed, by choice, at the people with the least power. Fix the aim and half the scandal never gets to happen.

So What Do You Do With This?

Short version, from a guy in Belgrano squinting at his own phone, counting little icons that suspiciously all look the same and seem to have been programmed by the same troll farm with the same subset of tools on how to properly manage your dough. We are all doomed.

If an app is paying you to keep money in it, ask who’s paying the app.

”Instant approval” means nobody looked. Not at you, and not at them.

Spread it out. One bank is a single point of failure. Paraguay just found out that 60% of a country can share one.

Ask your bank, your fintech, your regulator a very simple question: does your fancy AI audit anybody above the customer? Watch the silence.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

What is Ueno Bank?

Ueno Bank is a Paraguayan digital bank that grew out of an older finance company, got its banking license in 2023 and became the country’s largest by number of customers, claiming more than 60% of Paraguay’s adults. It is part of Grupo Vázquez. Paraguay’s central bank seized it in November 2026 after finding its capital had fallen below half the legal minimum.

Why was Paraguay’s president impeached?

Santiago Peña was impeached in November 2026 after Ueno Bank collapsed. Lawmakers alleged misconduct and a conflict of interest: Peña held a stake in Ueno’s holding company until 2025, government money flowed into the bank under his administration, and he appointed a former head of Ueno’s predecessor company to run the central bank that supervised it. He tried to resign, Congress refused and impeached him anyway. He has not been convicted of any crime.

Who is Paraguay’s president now?

Former Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in after the Senate’s impeachment vote and would serve the rest of the term, through August 2028.

Will Ueno customers get their money back?

The central bank said it began repaying depositors and planned to move accounts and any remaining good assets to other banks before liquidating the rest, including opening new accounts for about 280,000 people who banked only with Ueno. Timelines and how much each depositor recovers were still unclear at the time of writing.

Did AI cause the Ueno Bank collapse?

No. The reported cause is insider lending and bad accounting. AI is part of the story because Ueno marketed an AI assistant that analyzed customers’ in-app behavior to decide on credit, while the riskiest lending, to companies tied to its owners, went unchecked.

Are Argentine fintechs at risk of the same thing?

Different model, related problem. Most Argentine wallets don’t hold deposits the way Ueno did, but their loan books are in trouble: by March 2026 more than 10% of fintech and wallet lending was classified as unrecoverable, up from 2.6% a year earlier.

Back to the cornbread line from the top. Somewhere in Asunción tonight there’s an old man standing outside a locked branch, holding a phone with a smiley face on it, the little app still promising cash-back on his groceries.

The app knows what he had for lunch.

It just can’t tell him where his pension went. Nobody asked it to look.
