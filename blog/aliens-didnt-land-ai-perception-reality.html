Aliens Didn’t Land: AI, Perception and Friday the 13th

November 14, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog
Part of The Attention Machine  ·  Chapter 8 of 8 →

Friday the 13th, 3 A.M., and the Jellyfish Fleet Over Miami: How the Feed Sold Me an Alien Invasion, and What Reality Looks Like in 2036

Let me confess something up front, so you can feel superior for the rest of this post. Go on. Take a moment. Puff your chest. Polish your little monocle of reason.

Last night, at three in the morning, on Friday the 13th, I believed the aliens had landed.

Not fully. Not “call my mother and tell her I love her” believed. But enough. Enough that I was scrolling with the jaw-slack devotion of a medieval peasant watching a comet and wondering which neighbor to burn for it. I was sloshed on cheap port a friend had given me.

And the worst part? The aliens didn’t even have to show up. They had a publicist. The publicist was the algorithm.

The Week Everything Broke at Once

To be fair to my brain pan, the week had been an absolute carnival of catastrophe.

Nvidia’s board announced an independent investigation into its own founder and CEO, Jensen Huang, after reports he’d been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Huang admitted an affair. By Wednesday the Wall Street Journal was reporting the board was preparing to oust him. The company’s line: he’s staying, “without interruption.” The man in the leather jacket who sells the shovels for the entire AI gold rush was suddenly a soap opera, and the soap opera had a spy in it.

Outlook hiccuped, with mailbox access failing for chunks of users in Europe, the Middle East and Africa. A fireball streaked across the night sky over Florida’s Gulf Coast. A building collapsed in Brooklyn. A tanker collided with a cruise ship on the Mississippi in New Orleans. An explosion and fire tore through the center of Remedios de Escalada, back home in Argentina. And Musk’s Starship made it to orbit after a booster malfunction blew its double-catch attempt.

And then, Friday afternoon, the big one.

Ground Stop Fact On Friday, November 13, 2026, shortly after 3:30 p.m. Eastern, the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop for all U.S. airports after a communications failure hit every Air Route Traffic Control Center. More than 3,500 planes were already airborne and were allowed to land. Departures stayed frozen for over four hours, the longest national halt since September 2001 by Bloomberg’s count. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed an “incredibly old” air traffic control system and overloaded routers. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin added the single least calming sentence available to a government official: “You just can’t rule out anything at this point.”

You just can’t rule out anything. Thanks, Markwayne. Very helpful. That’s like a doctor walking out of your X-ray and saying “could be a cold, could be a tiny Satan.”

How the Feed Builds a Mothership Out of Spare Parts

Like most people who claim to be informed, I get my breaking news from X and Instagram, which is a bit like getting your nutrition from a vending machine at a bus station. It’s technically food. Something in there is technically a peanut.

And Instagram took that week, every outage and fireball and grounded plane, and stitched it into a single story. Florida was “assailed.” Fort Myers to Miami to Pompano Beach, asteroid-like streaks over the sky. Jellyfish ships, translucent and trailing tentacles, “spotted” over cities on four continents. Stephen King, the man who invented half the monsters under your bed, announcing that the aliens had descended on his accounts.

And here’s the kicker: King really did say it. As a joke. Which, if you think about it, is the most Stephen King thing that could happen: the master of horror tosses off a gag, and the feed serves it back to 3 a.m. me as a press release. The one real quote in the whole mothership was the punchline, and I was the one who didn’t get it.

Here’s the mechanics, and they’re dumb, which is what makes them work. The feed doesn’t need to lie to you. It needs to order things. Real fireball. Real ground stop. Real outage. Real CEO in a spy scandal. Then one fake jellyfish video, slotted in between them like a card shark palming an ace. Five true things lend their credibility to the sixth. Every reel you watch to the end tells the machine “more of this,” and the machine, a tireless and soulless butler, brings you more of this. More certain. More breathless. More tentacles.

By 3 a.m. I wasn’t consuming news. I was being fed, like a goose for foie gras, by a funnel with a recommendation engine.

STOP. Go Outside.

Pull the car over. Let’s do the math a ten-year-old could do.

If a fleet of jellyfish motherships were hovering over Miami, roughly six million people in the metro area would walk outside, look up, and point a phone at it. Every balcony in Brickell. Every cruise ship in the port. Every drunk on Ocean Drive. Every grandmother in Hialeah screaming at her grandson to get the good camera.

No government on Earth can black out a sky. You can censor a newspaper. You can pull a broadcast. You can lean on a platform. You cannot issue a gag order to the firmament. The ground stop itself proves the point: within minutes there were thousands of stranded passengers posting photos of departure boards from Atlanta to Seattle. The cover-up theory required a government so competent it could hide a mothership and so incompetent it couldn’t keep its routers from choking. Pick one.

Carl Sagan popularized a line for exactly this situation: “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.” My extraordinary evidence was a Reel with a dubstep soundtrack and the caption “THEY DON’T WANT YOU TO SEE THIS 👽.”

I’m not freaking Neil deGrasse Tyson. I don’t know orbital mechanics. But I know a parking lot, and I know that if the sky over a parking lot in Pompano is full of tentacles, someone from Pompano is going to post it with their actual face, their actual voice and a very Floridian amount of profanity.

Plato Called It, and He Didn’t Even Have Wi-Fi

Here’s what kept me up after the aliens went home. This isn’t new. This is the oldest question humans have, wearing a cheap new costume.

About 2,400 years ago, Plato wrote the allegory of the cave. Prisoners chained since birth, facing a wall. Behind them, a fire. Between the fire and the prisoners, people carry objects that throw shadows on the wall. The prisoners think the shadows are reality, because they’ve never seen anything else. If one escapes and comes back to describe the sun, the others want to kill him.

Swap the fire for a server farm. Swap the shadows for a vertical video feed. Swap the puppeteers for an engagement-optimization model trained on the reactions of three billion people. Swap the chains for… no, keep the chains. The chains are fine. The chains are the phone.

And it’s a whole lineage of the same panic:

- Zhuangzi, around the 4th century BC, dreamed he was a butterfly and woke unsure whether he was a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man.

- René Descartes, 1641, imagined an evil demon feeding him a false world through his senses, perfectly, so he could never catch the seams. He was describing a content team.

- George Berkeley argued that to be is to be perceived. Which, in 2026, is also the business model of every influencer alive.

- Jean Baudrillard, 1981, Simulacra and Simulation: the copy replaces the original until there’s no original left to check against, only maps with no territory. He called it the hyperreal. We call it the For You page.

Every one of these thinkers asked the same thing: how do you know what you know? For most of history the honest answer was “my eyes, my neighbors, and a priest I mostly trusted.” The eyes were the last line of defense. The eyes were the referee.

Last night, I didn’t use my eyes. I used my thumb. And my thumb, God bless it, is a dullard.

Self-Rebuttal: Humans Have Always Been Gullible, You Melodramatic Goose

Fair. Let me argue against myself before the top comment does.

Moon Hoax Fact In August 1835, the New York Sun ran a series claiming a famous astronomer had spotted life on the Moon through a giant telescope: forests, beaches, unicorn-like creatures, and winged humanoid “man-bats.” Readers ate it up and the paper’s circulation soared. No algorithm required. Just ink, a good illustrator and a public that wanted to believe.

And the 1938 War of the Worlds radio broadcast? The legend says mass panic in the streets. Historians now say the panic was wildly overstated, largely by newspapers eager to prove that radio, their new competitor, was dangerous and irresponsible. Meaning the most famous fake-news panic in history was partly… fake news about fake news. A snake eating its own tail, then posting the tail.

So yes. Humans have always swallowed nonsense whole. We’re born with a shovel for a mouth and a sieve for a brain.

What’s different isn’t the gullibility. It’s three things.

Scale. The Sun reached New Yorkers. The feed reaches everyone, at once, during the exact hour the FAA grounds the country.

Personalization. The Moon Hoax was one story for all readers. The feed builds a custom hoax for the specific 3 a.m. version of you, the one with low blood sugar and high suggestibility, and it learns from every second you linger.

The death of the referee. In 1835 you could eventually look through a telescope. In 2026, you can’t trust the photo from the telescope either.

What Reality Looks Like in 2036

Speculation ahead, clearly labeled, because I’m a novelist and not an oracle, and the oracles are mostly running ad networks anyway.

We’re maybe three or four years into the AI maelstrom. Last night, my perception got hijacked by a feed that mostly just rearranged real events, plus a couple of cheap fakes. No frontier model was needed to fool me. Now imagine 2036, when anyone can generate a live, multi-angle, geolocated, emotionally tuned video of anything, in seconds, for pennies.

The sky gets a verification badge. Content credentials already exist: cryptographic labels that record where a photo came from and how it was edited. In ten years, unlabeled footage might read the way an unsigned check reads today. “Raw camera, signed at capture” becomes the new “seeing is believing.” Which means the most valuable object in 2036 might be a dumb camera that can’t do anything except tell the truth.

The liar’s dividend pays out. Legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron named this one years ago: when fakes are everywhere, real evidence gets dismissed as fake too. The danger isn’t just that you’ll believe the jellyfish. It’s that when something real happens, a real mothership or a real crime on a real camera, half the planet will shrug and say “AI.” Truth won’t get killed. It’ll get ignored to death, which is worse, because there’s no body.

Reality fragments into subscriptions. Your feed and my feed already disagree about what happened this week. In 2036 they might disagree about the weather. Each of us living in a bespoke cave with a bespoke fire and a bespoke Plato, all of them selling ads.

And the cosmic punchline. If real aliens ever do show up, really, actually, tentacles and all… we won’t believe it. We’ll check the comments. We’ll ask the chatbot. We’ll wait for a verified account. The first contact in human history will get a Community Note, and the Note will say “missing context.” And somewhere out past Jupiter an ancient intelligence, older than our sun, will watch the whole species squint at its arrival and decide to come back in a thousand years, when we’ve finished arguing.

I wrote about the Fermi Paradox here before, the silence of the universe. Here’s a new candidate filter, free of charge: maybe every civilization eventually builds a machine that tells it whatever it wants to hear, and nobody ever looks up again.

So What Do We Do, Besides Panic Beautifully?

Nothing heroic. A few small, boring, unglamorous habits. Boring is the point. Boring is load-bearing.

1. Go outside. If the claim is about the sky, look at the sky. If it’s about your city, call someone in your city. The body is still the best fact-checker ever built. It’s slow and it hates leaving the couch, but it’s never been bought by an advertiser.

2. Count the sources that aren’t the feed. If the only evidence is reels, and the reels cite each other, you’re standing in a hall of mirrors yelling “who’s there?”

3. Notice the hour. Nothing true needs to be believed at 3 a.m. It’ll still be true at 9.

4. Distrust what flatters your fear. The feed knows what you’re afraid of better than your therapist does, and your therapist doesn’t get paid per minute of panic.

The next morning I told my best friend the whole thing. His response: “Dude, that, and did you see that Jeffrey Epstein is alive…” In hindsight, I shouldn’t have gone for comfort to the friend who’s even weirder than me.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Did aliens cause the FAA ground stop on November 13, 2026?

No. The FAA and the Department of Transportation attributed it to a communications failure across all Air Route Traffic Control Centers, which the Transportation Secretary blamed on aging equipment and overloaded routers. Flights already in the air landed normally.

How long did the nationwide ground stop last?

More than four hours, starting shortly after 3:30 p.m. Eastern on Friday, November 13, 2026. Bloomberg called it the longest national halt since the September 2001 attacks.

Was Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang ousted?

As of this writing, no. Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation on November 8, 2026, and the Wall Street Journal reported the board was preparing to remove him. The company has said he remains CEO.

Why does the Instagram algorithm push conspiracy content?

Recommendation systems rank content by what keeps people watching. Fear, novelty and mystery hold attention, so content with those qualities spreads, whether or not it’s true.

What is the liar’s dividend?

A term coined by legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron for the way widespread deepfakes let people dismiss real evidence as fake.

What is Plato’s allegory of the cave, and what does it have to do with AI?

Plato described prisoners who mistake shadows on a wall for reality. Algorithmic feeds work the same way: you see a curated projection of events, chosen by a system you can’t see, and it can feel more real than the world outside.

How can I tell if a viral video is real?

Check whether independent people in the location are posting their own footage, look for content credentials or original sources, search for reports from established outlets, and be most suspicious of anything that confirms your fears late at night.

So. The aliens didn’t land. The FAA’s routers choked, a CEO’s love life became a national security matter, a rock from space burned up over the Gulf, and a machine in a data center decided my 3 a.m. self would watch tentacles until dawn. It was right.

Go outside. Look up. If the mothership’s there, film it with your real face and your real voice and your full Floridian vocabulary. If it isn’t, put the phone in a drawer and go to bed.

The aliens can wait. They’ve had thirteen point eight billion years. They can handle one more night.
