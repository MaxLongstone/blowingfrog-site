“A Drunk in a Sombrero Showed Me God, and God Was a Picture of Two Guys Punching Each Other”

November 13, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

Let me tell you who I was in 2021.

I was a mayonnaise sandwich. White bread. Crusts on. No lettuce, because lettuce is a lifestyle and I didn’t have one. I was a stable, fairly mature man. I paid things on time. I had opinions about thread count. COVID had not yet f@$ked me up, and I mean that clinically, as in the noggin furniture was still where the decorator left it.

For all intents and purposes, I was dull as a mayo sandwich. And the mayo was light.

Then came the friend.

The Friend Everybody Has (and If You Don’t, Bad News, It’s You)

You know the type.

The type who kicks open your dorm room in Boston wearing nothing but a sombrero, swinging a bottle of José Cuervo like an altar boy with a censer, and announces with the confidence of a cult leader on his third wife: “Dude. We need to go to Mexico. The señoritas are fine.”

No pants. Sombrero. Liquor. A plan. That’s the whole résumé, and somehow it’s enough to run a small country.

That friend now goes by BatAI Kino, and I’m going to say nice things about him later, so enjoy the next few paragraphs, buddy, because they’re the last ones where you come off like a public nuisance.

Every man has one of these. If you’re reading this and you can’t think of yours, put the bottle down and check what you’re wearing on your head.

The Picture

Sometime in 2022 he sends me an image.

Midjourney. Version 2, I think, which in AI years is the Cretaceous. For the kids in the back: Midjourney V2 rendered hands like a bag of carrots and faces like someone described a face to a sponge.

The picture: Mad Max going fist for fist with Batman.

The leather jacket. The cape. Two knuckles meeting in a smear of orange light. One of them had six fingers, possibly seven, possibly a thumb that had ideas. The background was the color of a burn ward. It was, by any objective standard, garbage.

It was also the most beautiful garbage I had ever seen.

And then he said the sentence. I remember the punctuation. I remember the little dramatic pause where he let it breathe, like a man who knows he’s about to ruin your life and wants to savor it:

“And I did that with just, like, f@$king goddamn words.”

That day, I saw God.

Not a metaphor. The bush was burning. The bush had a Discord server. The bush was taking requests in a channel called #newbies-12 and the commandments came out in a grid of four, slightly cursed, upscale optional.

Five Years Later, Look at Me

Here I am. Doing this for a living.

Not as a hobby. Not as a midlife crisis with a GPU. As a living, with invoices and clients and a business card that would have made 2021 me spit out his sandwich.

And what does doing this for a living actually look like, on a Tuesday night, at an hour when decent people are asleep?

It looks like me hunched over a laptop studying how in the holy hell Godzilla Minus One got made on a budget reported under $15 million, and what lessons I can steal from it. With a knife. In an alley.

It is, and continues to be, distressing on a spiritual level.

My soul filed a complaint with management. Management is a drunk in a sombrero. The complaint was used to light something.

Now, I’m going to tell you about my friend’s zombie movie. But to get there we have to take a hard left into a filthy theater on 42nd Street in 1974, then a short ride to Pittsburgh in 1968, and THEN we can talk about the lizard. You thought this was a straight road. It’s a cloverleaf designed by a drunk. You know which drunk.

Keep your feet off the floor. It’s sticky for reasons.

STOP.

Grindhouse: A Church With Sticky Floors

A grindhouse was a cheap movie theater, the classic ones jammed along 42nd Street in New York, that ran exploitation pictures back to back, all day, all night. Kung fu. Biker gangs. Cannibals. Women in prison. Car chases shot by a guy who stole the car. The prints were scratched to hell, reels went missing, and nobody cared, because you were paying almost nothing to sit in the dark with strangers and watch something nobody else would show you.

It was church for people who’d been thrown out of church.

Which brings us to the high priest.

Quentin Tarantino didn’t just watch these movies. He memorized them, ate them, metabolized them and sweated them back out as Pulp Fiction, Kill Bill and every other film where somebody gives a monologue about a cheeseburger before shooting a man. He is the grindhouse kid who grew up and got the keys to the studio.

And my friend, the sombrero prophet, the man with no pants and a plan, is obsessed with this stuff. We were both film buffs, met at the academy, both convinced that Tarantino and Rodriguez were the bomb before it was a popular opinion. When Grindhouse came out in 2007, something in us recognized it like a frequency: oh, there’s a whole world of movies made on pocket lint and pure nerve that the mainstream swallowed whole. Neither of us had a name for it before. After that, we needed to have seen all of it, retroactively, immediately.

So of course, in 2007, Tarantino and Robert Rodriguez decided to recreate the whole experience.

Grindhouse Fact Grindhouse opened April 6, 2007, as a three-hour-plus double feature. Rodriguez’s Planet Terror (a gooey, infected-zombie bloodbath) and Tarantino’s Death Proof (Kurt Russell, a stunt car and a very bad attitude), stitched together with fake trailers from Rob Zombie, Edgar Wright and Eli Roth, plus a contest-winning Hobo with a Shotgun trailer by Jason Eisener in some markets. Two of those fake trailers later became real movies: Machete (2010) and Hobo with a Shotgun (2011). Eli Roth turned Thanksgiving into a real feature in 2023. The fake trailers outlived the real movie. Think about that.

They scratched the prints on purpose. They faked missing reels. It was a love letter, written in blood and motor oil, to movies that cost pocket lint.

The love letter cost somewhere between $53 and $67 million, depending on who’s counting.

It opened to around $11.5 million on Easter weekend and ended with about $25 million in the U.S. A tribute to cheap movies, made expensive, died expensive. Tarantino has said in interviews that the flop shook his confidence. Picture that. The man who made Hitler explode in a burning cinema, rattled by the Easter Bunny.

Film Threat ran the math back then and put the budget at roughly 530 times what a typical old grindhouse picture cost. You can’t buy the smell of a sticky floor. You can try. It’ll run you sixty-something million and they’ll still split your movie in half overseas so normal people will watch it.

Which they did. Outside the U.S., Planet Terror and Death Proof went out as two separate films.

Hold that number. Sixty-seven million. We’re coming back for it.

The Dead Have Always Worked Cheap

Here is the beautiful, filthy secret of the zombie movie: it is the cheapest monster ever invented.

A vampire needs a castle. A werewolf needs a transformation scene that costs more than your house. A kaiju needs a city to step on.

A zombie needs a field, some grey makeup, a farmhouse and a dozen of your friends willing to shamble for sandwiches. Mayo optional. The dead don’t unionize.

Zombie Fact George A. Romero shot Night of the Living Dead (1968) around Pittsburgh for roughly $114,000, with local investors, a crew of commercial and industrial filmmakers, and extras who were mostly regular people from the area. It defined the modern zombie. A rounding error built the genre.

That’s the whole lineage. Romero in a Pittsburgh field. The Italians knocking off Romero with even less money and more eyeballs. Rodriguez dousing a whole town in goo for Planet Terror. Zombies are the genre where poor kids get to make the real thing, because the monster is just a person who stopped caring about their posture.

And that, ladies and degenerates, is why my friend made a zombie movie.

So here’s the shout-out, and I mean every word of it, which should worry him.

The sombrero prophet makes movies now, under the banner BatAI Kino (@bataikino on Instagram, go follow him, I’ll wait). And he just put out a zombie short called DUST!

The exclamation point is his. He earned it.

The man who taught me AI art with a picture of two guys punching each other, seven fingers between them, went out and made an actual film. With dead people in it. And it looks like a print somebody dug out of a drive-in’s dumpster in 1976, which is the highest compliment I know how to pay.

Look at that title card. Mustard-yellow slab letters, scorched and blotched like they were stored next to a radiator since the Carter administration, floating over a cloud of dirt. That’s not a font. That’s a smell.

A garbage truck the color of a hospital hallway, rust breaking out on it like a rash, hauling down a dirt road inside its own dust storm. Every grindhouse needs a vehicle with a criminal record. This one has priors.

Behind the wheel, a man with a mustache that has its own pension plan, smoking like the cigarette owes him money, staring down the road with the patience of a guy who has seen the end of the world and found it underwhelming.

And then. The dog. In the side mirror. Just sitting there, with eyes like two high beams from hell.

Objects in mirror are closer, and possibly worse, than they appear.

A garbage truck driver. A dusty road. Something wrong with the dead, something worse with the dog, and a fly doing more narrative work than most supporting cast in a $67 million movie. It’s messy, odd, and really well made. Good weird.

And here’s the kicker. Every frame of it is AI. All of it. Full on, every model under the sun, the man ran the whole zoo. The truck, the mustache, the dog, the dust. The sombrero prophet went back to the burning bush and ordered the entire menu.

Hunger. You either have it or you fake it. Audiences smell the fake from the parking lot.

Godzilla Minus One: The $15 Million Lizard That Ate Hollywood’s Lunch

Let me give you the numbers straight, because the numbers are the joke.

Godzilla Minus One (2023) was written and directed by Takashi Yamazaki, who also did the visual effects. Not supervised from a yacht. Did them. His studio, Shirogumi, handled the work out of its Chōfu studio.

Kaiju Fact The film’s budget was widely reported at under $15 million, with most estimates landing between $10 and $15 million. The VFX team was about 35 artists. They delivered roughly 610 visual effects shots (some outlets count 620). It took about eight months. It made over $105 million worldwide. And at the 2024 Oscars it won Best Visual Effects, the first Godzilla film to win an Oscar in the franchise’s seventy years, and the first Japanese film to take that category. Yamazaki became only the second director to win the VFX Oscar, after Stanley Kubrick for 2001: A Space Odyssey.

Read that again. Thirty-five people. A big Marvel movie can have thousands of VFX artists in the credits, spread across a dozen vendors on four continents, half of them working Christmas.

And here’s the part that makes me want to lie down on the kitchen floor: one of the four people who took home that Oscar, compositor Tatsuji Nojima, was 25 at the time, and he got into the water simulations because he’d been messing around with them at home, for fun. The ocean that Godzilla rises out of, the one that makes you physically cold in the theater, started as a young guy’s hobby.

The ocean was a hobby. Let that marinate.

I’m not a Japanese line producer. I can’t read a Toho budget sheet, and I couldn’t tell you how they handle overtime in Chōfu without a translator, a lawyer and a stiff drink. But I can read a screen, and I can read a credit list, and the math is obscene.

Also worth saying out loud: Yamazaki’s team didn’t use generative AI to make it. This is not an AI story. It’s a constraint story. Which is exactly why it matters to someone who does make things with AI.

Five Things I’m Stealing From a Radioactive Lizard

1. One brain holds the knife. Yamazaki wrote it, directed it and ran the effects. That means the person deciding what the monster does is the same person deciding what the monster costs. No committee of fourteen vice presidents in a glass room asking whether Godzilla can be “more relatable.” Every shot goes through one skull. The skull says no to most of them. That’s the savings.

2. Spend shots like a widow spends sugar. 610 shots. That’s not a lot for a monster movie. The film rations its monster, and every appearance lands like a punch because you’ve been starved. Spielberg learned this when his mechanical shark kept breaking on Jaws, and he’s apparently a big fan of this lizard. The cheapest effect in the world is the one you don’t show. The second cheapest is the one you show once, perfectly.

3. Hire the kid who does it for fun. The water guy was playing with oceans on his own time. You cannot pay for that. You can only find it, feed it and get out of its way. Every studio on Earth is trying to buy passion with a benefits package. Shirogumi found a guy who was already making oceans for free and handed him the Pacific.

4. Story is the cheapest effect in the building. The heart of Minus One isn’t the atomic breath. It’s Kōichi Shikishima, a kamikaze pilot who didn’t go through with it, carrying that shame through the ruins of postwar Tokyo. Guilt costs nothing. Grief costs nothing. A man trying to forgive himself costs nothing. And it makes every dollar of the lizard work three times harder, because you actually give a sh!t who gets stepped on.

5. You can’t buy cheap. And here’s where the sixty-seven million comes back. Grindhouse spent a fortune to imitate poverty. Godzilla Minus One was made under real limits and turned them into a style. One is a rich kid in distressed jeans that cost $400. The other is a kid whose jeans are just like that. Audiences know the difference. They always know the difference.

Okay, Smartass, Here’s Why I’m Wrong

Two objections, and they’re good ones, so I’ll take them before the comment section does.

One: the budget comparison is apples to sushi. A Japanese production budget isn’t a Hollywood budget. Labor costs differ, Shirogumi is an in-house shop that doesn’t mark up like an outside vendor, and the cheapness itself raised eyebrows. When the “35 artists” number went around, people asked whether a small team on a tiny budget meant somebody was getting squeezed, and Yamazaki publicly pushed back on that framing. Fair. “They did it for $15 million” can quietly become “everyone should do it for $15 million,” and that’s how you end up with artists sleeping under their desks. A miracle is not a business model.

Two: cheap tools don’t make good movies. This one’s aimed straight at my own industry. In July 2026, Neill Blomkamp, the District 9 guy, released Nightborne, a 13-minute zombie short made entirely with AI video tools. Critics and viewers went after it hard, calling it slop, pointing at the visual inconsistencies and the disjointed camera work. If an Oscar-nominated director with a cool premise can get roasted for this, the tools are not the talent. Words make pictures now. Words don’t make taste.

Which is exactly why DUST! matters to me. Same family of tools as Nightborne. All AI, every model he could get his paws on. And it doesn’t look like slop. It looks like a drive-in print with a grudge, because somebody with taste was holding the wheel. The tools didn’t bring the taste. He did.

Both true. Neither one changes the lesson. A cheap camera is a door, not a director. The lizard proved you can walk through the door. Nobody promised you’d know where you were going once you got inside.

Back to the Sandwich

So here I am, five years out from the sombrero.

I’m still a mayonnaise sandwich, honestly. White bread. Crusts on. Except now there’s a monster in it, punching up through the top slice, and the mayo is everywhere, and I have to explain to clients that yes, the monster is the business model.

My friend, meanwhile, went from a picture of two guys punching each other with seven fingers between them to a real zombie movie with a real story about real dead people. From six fingers to DUST! in four years. Romero would buy him a beer. Tarantino would buy him a beer, then explain the beer to him for forty minutes.

And I keep thinking about that sentence. The one that broke my brain open like an egg on a hot sidewalk:

”And I did that with just, like, f@$king goddamn words.”

He was right. He was drunk, pantless and spiritually unsupervised, and he was right. It was words. It’s still words. The lizard was words first, in Yamazaki’s head, before it was 610 shots. DUST! was words first. Grindhouse was words first, and then it was sixty-seven million dollars, and the words drowned.

Keep the words. Starve the budget. Feed the monster.

Distressing on a spiritual level. Every day. So peachy.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

What was Godzilla Minus One’s budget?

It was widely reported at under $15 million, with most estimates between $10 and $15 million. It grossed over $105 million worldwide and won the 2024 Oscar for Best Visual Effects.

How many VFX artists worked on Godzilla Minus One?

About 35 artists at the Japanese studio Shirogumi, delivering roughly 610 effects shots over around eight months. Director Takashi Yamazaki also served as the film’s VFX supervisor.

Did Godzilla Minus One use AI?

No generative AI was used to make the visual effects. The team relied on conventional tools such as Maya for animation, Houdini for water simulation and Nuke for compositing.

Why did Grindhouse flop?

Grindhouse (2007) cost a reported $53 to $67 million and made about $25 million in the U.S. Common explanations include its three-hour-plus runtime, an Easter weekend release and audiences who didn’t understand the double-feature concept. Outside the U.S., Planet Terror and Death Proof were released as separate films.

Is Planet Terror a zombie movie?

Yes, more or less. Robert Rodriguez’s half of Grindhouse is a gory horror comedy about survivors fighting people turned into zombie-like creatures by a leaked bioweapon gas.

How much did Night of the Living Dead cost?

George A. Romero’s 1968 original was made for roughly $114,000 around Pittsburgh, and it defined the modern zombie film.
