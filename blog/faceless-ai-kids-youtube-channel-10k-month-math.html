Faceless AI Kids YouTube Channel: The $10K Math Is Fake

October 26, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog
Part of The Attention Machine  ·  Chapter 7 of 7 →

The Man in My Phone Wants to Sell Me a Money Printer, and for Some Reason He Isn’t Using It

I was watching Cory Doctorow trash-talk Mark Zuckerberg, which is one of my preferred forms of prayer, and right after it came what can only be described as the finest Halloween makeup tutorial on the internet. The episode was the nurse from Silent Hill. The faceless one. Gauze, a head like a swollen sack, a twitch in every joint, a woman turning her own face into a wet thing with no features and doing it with the calm hands of a surgeon.

One man explaining how the platform eats you. One woman deleting her face with a sponge. And then the algorithm, with the timing of a cabal and the sense of humor of a Lovecraftian intern, served me the third act.

Another man with no face. Or rather, selling one.

He looked into my phone with the steady sincerity of Matthew McConaughey selling a Lincoln to a horse, and he told me that with Claude, a little AI elbow grease and zero face on camera, I could build a YouTube channel for kids and pull down an alternative revenue stream of ten thousand dollars a month.

Ten thousand. A month. For cartoons. Made by a robot. Watched by toddlers.

And then, the way the feed works, there were a thousand of him. Same pitch, different haircut. Faceless channels. AI automation. Passive income. Swipe, there he is again, now in a rented Lambo. Swipe, now in a rented kitchen. Swipe, now in a rented Dubai. It was less a feed than a plague, the biblical kind, with frogs, except every frog had a ring light and a discount code. (As a man whose company is literally named Blowing Frog, I took this personally.)

Here’s the thing. I know this topic. I build AI pipelines for a living. I’ve watched what these channels earn and what they cost. And the numbers this man was waving around did not add up.

Not “a little optimistic.” Not “results may vary.”

They did not ADD UP.

So let’s add them. Out loud, like grown-ups, with a calculator and a Hillbilly Margarita.

For the uninitiated: a Hillbilly Margarita is tequila. That’s the recipe. Straight up, no lime, no salt, no little umbrella, and not the good kind. The kind that strips the paint off your tongue, then the varnish, then a thin layer of your immortal soul, and calls it a garnish.

Exhibit A: A Man, a Microphone and the Number 10,000

I’m not going to name him. He’s not special. That’s the point. He is a genre, the way “guy at the bar who almost went pro” is a genre, and if I named him, three more would grow back in the space, like a hydra with a Shopify store.

The pitch, distilled, goes like this. One: pick a kids niche, because kids rewatch everything and kids don’t skip. Two: let AI write the scripts, generate the visuals, do the voices. Three: upload daily. Four: profit. Five, and this is where the music swells: join my program and I’ll show you the exact system.

Notice that step five is the only step with a price tag.

Hold that thought, because we’re going to take this car somewhere it didn’t expect to go.

STOP.

You thought this was going to be a rant about AI slop. It is, partly. But we’re going to pull the car over, get out, pop the hood and find that the engine is not a YouTube channel. It never was. Bring a flashlight. Bring a tetanus shot.

The Math They Don’t Put on the Slide

Start with the money YouTube actually pays.

A YouTube channel earns ad revenue only after it gets into the YouTube Partner Program. The full monetization tier today requires 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 public watch hours in the past 12 months (or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days). In August 2026 YouTube announced that from February 1, 2027, new applicants will need 8,000 watch hours or 20 million Shorts views. The bar to get paid is DOUBLING. Nobody in my feed mentioned that, which is odd, because it’s sort of the first thing.

Once you’re in, creators keep 55% of long-form ad revenue and 45% on Shorts. The number that tells you what a channel actually takes home is RPM: revenue per thousand views, after YouTube’s cut.

And here is where the kids niche comes apart in your hands like a wet paper plate.

Guru Fact Content aimed at children must be designated “made for kids,” a rule that has applied since January 2020, after Google and YouTube paid $170 million to settle COPPA allegations with the FTC and the New York Attorney General. Made-for-kids videos cannot show personalized ads. Only contextual ones. According to AIR Media-Tech’s data on 300 monetized channels (May 2025 to May 2026), the median RPM for made-for-kids content is $0.33. Education and science sits at $10.22. Gaming at $2.05. Kids content earns about 3% of what education earns per view.

Thirty-three cents. Per thousand views.

So let’s do the arithmetic the man in my phone skipped.

$10,000 a month ÷ $0.33 per 1,000 views = about 30.3 million views a month.

That’s roughly one million views a day. Every day. Thirty days running. No holidays, no Sundays, no “the algorithm was weird this week.” A million toddlers, give or take, sitting through your robot nursery rhyme every single day so you can pay rent.

Be generous. Say you’re in the top tier and pull a dollar RPM. That’s still 10 million views a month, 333,000 a day, from a channel that, per the pitch, you started last Tuesday with a chatbot.

I’m not freaking Warren Buffett. I don’t do compound interest in my head at parties. But I can divide, and the division says the dream needs the audience of a mid-sized nation. Pour another Hillbilly Margarita. The next part is worse.

Everything Made for Kids Is Missing a Button

It gets worse, because “made for kids” doesn’t just shrink the paycheck. It removes the tools you’d use to grow.

On made-for-kids videos, YouTube switches off comments, the notification bell, end screens and cards, channel memberships, Super Chat, and saving to Watch Later. Read that list again with a growth hat on. No bell means no “new video” ping. No end screens means no “watch this next.” No comments means no community, no feedback, no signal. No memberships means no second revenue line.

You are building a store with no sign, no door, no windows and no cash register except the one bolted to the ceiling, where a man named YouTube decides whether to open it.

Your only path to a million views a day is the recommendation algorithm choosing you, over and over, against tens of thousands of other channels that bought the same course from the same man.

Which brings us to the part where the man’s own platform has already written the obituary.

YouTube Already Wrote the Obituary

On July 15, 2025, YouTube renamed its “repetitious content” monetization policy to ”inauthentic content,” to make clear it covers mass-produced and repetitive videos.

On July 16, 2026, it went further. YouTube’s trust and safety chief, Matt Halprin, laid out three categories of content that can’t be monetized: generic, repetitive or template-based content; content designed to be distressing or emotionally manipulative; and AI personas discussing sensitive topics like finance. Channels with too much of any of the three lose monetization. AI is still allowed. Cookie cutters are not.

Look at those three buckets and then look at the pitch in my feed.

A daily kids channel made from the same AI template, by a person who learned the template from a course that also sold it to ten thousand other people, is the dictionary definition of bucket one. Halprin even said tutorials could fall under the policy when they just reproduce what’s already prevalent on the platform. The guru’s own video is on the endangered list. It’s a snake eating its own tutorial.

And the kids corner specifically is on fire. In February 2026, a New York Times investigation found YouTube’s algorithm serving AI-generated videos to accounts set up as children’s, and YouTube cut those accounts off from ad revenue and YouTube Kids. More than 200 organizations and experts, including Jonathan Haidt, Fairplay and the American Federation of Teachers, signed a letter to Google and YouTube demanding bans on AI content for young viewers. CEO Neal Mohan named managing AI slop a top priority for 2026.

So the business plan is: enter the lowest-paying category on the platform, which is also the most legally radioactive category on the platform, using the exact production method the platform has publicly promised to demonetize, while the press and a few hundred child-development experts watch with binoculars.

That’s not a side hustle. That’s skydiving into a volcano with a parachute you bought from the volcano.

Guru Fact A 2024 academic study of AI-generated children’s videos on YouTube (Mintz and Bozzi, Federal University of Minas Gerais, presented through the Association of Internet Researchers) found that most of them didn’t get high view counts. It also turned up, as a secondary finding, numerous tutorial-style videos promoting how to monetize AI content. The kids’ cartoons were losing. The videos about the kids’ cartoons were the growth sector.

The Rich Don’t Do Tutorials

Here’s the part I can speak to from my own life.

I’ve met people who make five thousand dollars a day. Ten thousand a day. And one who makes that look like a tip jar.

I was working for a tech company and the job took me to Dubai. New client walks in. The man is, and I need you to hold these two words in the same hand without dropping either, an influencer journalist. With bodyguards. Freaking BODYGUARDS. Plural. To a meet and greet with a tech company.

I had no idea who he was. Everyone else in the room did. You could feel it, the way you feel a dog that’s decided not to bite you yet.

After the meet and greet, I did what any self-respecting professional does. I Googled him.

Net worth: over 250 million dollars. According to the internet, which has never once lied about anything.

Trust me. That man did not have an Instagram feed selling snake oil. He didn’t have time. He had bodyguards to feed.

And the main thing about that personality, the thing that unites all of them, is this: they don’t waste a minute making videos for Instagram, TikTok or YouTube telling you how they did it. They leverage their money. They hire. They buy the next thing. And they never, ever, tell you the secret sauce.

Why? Because money you earn is money taken from them.

Now, a quick honesty patch on that, because a sharp reader will get there first. YouTube’s long-form ad money isn’t a fixed pot that shrinks every time somebody new shows up. Advertisers bid on impressions; the pie grows and shrinks with the ad market. Shorts revenue, though, IS pooled and split by share of views, so there it’s literally zero-sum. And even on long-form, the logic holds, just one layer down: same niche, same toddlers, same handful of kid-safe advertisers, same algorithm slots. If the man in my phone really had a million-views-a-day kids machine, every student he trains is a competitor for his slot in the feed.

Coca-Cola doesn’t run a webinar on the formula. KFC doesn’t sell a masterclass on the eleven herbs and spices. And the guy with the money printer doesn’t sell you the printer.

He prints. Then he hires two large men to stand next to the printer.

Unless, and here’s the hinge on which this whole rotten door swings, the printer doesn’t work. And the webinar does.

Sam Brannan Sold the Pans

In 1848, a San Francisco newspaperman and shopkeeper named Sam Brannan heard there was gold at Sutter’s Mill. He didn’t grab a pan and head for the river. He bought every pick, shovel and pan he could find, stocked his store, and then, the story goes, ran through the streets of San Francisco waving a bottle of gold dust and shouting about gold on the American River.

The rush was on. Brannan had bought his pans for twenty cents apiece. He sold them for fifteen dollars. He became California’s first millionaire.

The miners, broadly speaking, did not.

You don’t mine the gold. You mine the miners.

That’s the oldest grift in the gold country and it has never once gone out of style. You don’t mine the gold. You mine the miners. The guru in my phone is Sam Brannan with a ring light, running through the streets of Instagram shouting “Gold! Gold from the American River! Also, the American River is a faceless AI kids channel, and the river is closing in February, and I have twelve seats left in my cohort!”

Gold Rush Fact Brannan died in 1889 in San Diego, broke. Before you take that as cosmic justice, note that it took him forty years and the miners were broke by Thursday. The universe is not a refund department.

So Who’s the Customer? Look Down.

Here is the whole post in one comparison. If you remember nothing else, steal this.

To make $10,000 a month from a made-for-kids channel at median RPM, you need about 30 million views.

To make $10,000 a month selling a course about it at, say, $997 a seat, you need eleven buyers.

Eleven. A dinner party. A minivan with a third row.

Thirty million toddler views, or eleven hopeful adults. You’re a rational businessman. Which one do you chase?

That’s why these videos persist. Their business isn’t the YouTube ad. It isn’t the channel. It isn’t Claude, which, for the record, writes words and can’t produce a finished cartoon on its own, so the “with Claude” part is doing a lot of costume work. Their business is you. The product is the pipeline, the “system,” the coaching calls, the Discord, the mastermind, the done-for-you upgrade, the next tier. The YouTube channel is the bait. You are the fish. And the hook, to finish the metaphor until it bleeds, is your own Tuesday-night longing for a way out.

It also explains the pyramid shape. Not a pyramid scheme in the legal sense, necessarily. But look at how it grows: the student who doesn’t make money from the channel discovers he can make money by making videos about making money from the channel. Now he’s a guru. Now he has students. The kids’ cartoons sit at the bottom of the whole structure like a foundation nobody visits, a few thousand views each, holding up a cathedral of webinars.

And this isn’t my theory. The FTC has been suing this exact shape for two years.

Growth Cave (sued March 2025): allegedly took about $50 million from consumers since 2020 with promises of big income, selling its main program through YouTube ads. The FTC’s complaint says the founders posted videos of their lavish lifestyles as proof the program worked, and that those lifestyles were funded by the consumers who lost money. Read that sentence twice. The proof was paid for by the victims. One alleged upfront charge: $6,800. The operation reportedly rebranded into an AI-flavored program called PassiveApps.

FBA Machine / Passive Scaling: promised guaranteed income from online stores run on ”AI-powered software.” The FTC says it cost consumers more than $15.9 million. The operator agreed in July 2025 to a permanent ban on selling business opportunities.

Ecommerce Empire Builders: sold an ”AI-powered Ecommerce Empire,” with training programs at almost $2,000 and done-for-you stores for tens of thousands. Banned from selling business opportunities in May 2025.

Click Profit: pitched an ”AI supercomputer” behind online storefronts. At least $14 million, per the FTC.

Different products. Same machine. AI in the brochure, income in the headline, a lifestyle in the background, and the only person reliably getting paid is the one holding the microphone.

And the species breeds. Swipe once and you’ll meet his cousins. The real estate Renaissance man, who’ll sell you the secret websites for finding government liens, as if the county clerk ran a speakeasy with a password. The Claude cognoscenti, robed in prompt engineering, who hand you the magic bullet: one prompt, one weekend, one new life. The Etsy expert with the right dropship empire, which is to say a warehouse on the far side of the planet and a little paper heart that says HANDMADE.

Different lures. Same teeth.

The rubes used to be gulled by a man with a folding table and three walnut shells. Now he has a podcast setup and a scheduling link. Same walnuts. Same pea. The pea, as ever, is in his pocket.

In Fairness to the Shovel Salesmen

Let me argue against myself, because the top comment will, and I’d rather it said something new.

”Some faceless channels really do make money.” True. A few do. Real, sustained, faceless channels exist, many of them with actual human writers, editors and a point of view. The academic study above found the occasional AI kids video that broke out. Exceptions are real. They’re also exactly what a lottery advertises. Showing me the winner is not the same as showing me the odds.

”Teaching is a legitimate business.” Also true, and I’ll defend it to the grave. Plenty of people sell honest courses. The line isn’t teaching. The line is the earnings claim. “Here’s how to edit faster” is a class. “Here’s how to make $10K a month” is a promise, and under the FTC’s own rules, a promise about income has to be typical and backed up. The second you sell a number, show me the median student’s number. Not your best one. The median.

”Luis, you build AI pipelines and sell them. Aren’t you a shovel salesman?” Not really… I don’t sell fool’s gold. I don’t make promises. I just sell what folks ask me for. “Hey, I want a YouTube channel for kids!” “Yes, sir”… I never ask why? Or what their objectives are?

”YouTube is cracking down already, so what’s the problem?” YouTube is cracking down on the cartoons. Nobody is cracking down on the commercial for the cartoons.

What the Platforms Should Actually Do

YouTube has already built the logic. It just pointed it at the wrong end of the pipe.

The July 2026 policy refuses to pay AI personas talking about finance. Fine. But the guy in my feed is a real human face making specific income claims about a business opportunity, which is the more dangerous version, and it sails right through. YouTube labels synthetic content. Instagram labels AI-made images. Neither labels a stranger telling you he’ll make you ten grand a month.

Here’s what a crackdown on the commercial would look like, and none of it requires new science:

1. An earnings-claim label. Any video that promises a specific income from a business opportunity gets a visible tag, the way altered or synthetic content does now.

2. No paid promotion of income claims without disclosure. If you run ads promising $10K a month, the ad links to the typical-earnings figure, or it doesn’t run. Growth Cave allegedly built a $50 million machine on YouTube ads. The ad system is the front door. Lock it.

3. Treat repeat offenders like slop farms. YouTube already removes entire channels from monetization when too much of their content is templated. A channel whose whole catalog is “make money with AI” funnels into a paid program is a template too. Same rule, same consequence.

4. Kill the “made for kids” money-hack framing at the source. If YouTube is serious about protecting toddlers from AI slop, then the videos that recruit the slop factories are part of the supply chain.

The platform isn’t helpless. It knows exactly who’s buying ads, who’s linking to checkout pages and whose “free training” ends in a sales call. It just hasn’t decided that you are worth protecting from the people who think you’re the product.

Cory Doctorow, the man I was watching when this whole descent began, has a word for what happens when a platform stops serving its users once they’re locked in. The word is enshittification. I’ll leave it with him. He earned it.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Can a faceless AI kids YouTube channel make $10,000 a month?

It’s possible on paper and rare in practice. At the 2026 median made-for-kids RPM of about $0.33, $10,000 a month requires roughly 30 million views a month, about a million a day. Even at a $1 RPM it’s 10 million views a month.

Why do made-for-kids videos pay so little?

Since January 2020, after a $170 million COPPA settlement between Google/YouTube and the FTC and New York Attorney General, made-for-kids content can’t show personalized ads, only contextual ones. Comments, the notification bell, end screens and memberships are also switched off.

Does YouTube demonetize AI-generated videos?

Not for using AI. YouTube demonetizes “inauthentic content”: mass-produced, repetitive or template-based videos, distressing content made to chase views, and AI personas discussing sensitive topics like finance. The policy was renamed in July 2025 and clarified in July 2026.

What are the YouTube Partner Program requirements?

Today: 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 public watch hours in 12 months, or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days. From February 1, 2027, new applicants will need 8,000 watch hours or 20 million Shorts views.

How do I spot a YouTube automation or AI income scam?

A specific monthly income promise, a lifestyle used as proof, a “free training” that ends in a sales call, urgency (“twelve seats left”), and no typical-earnings figure. Ask for the median student’s results. If they can’t show them, you have your answer.

Has the FTC acted against AI money-making courses?

Yes. Recent cases include Growth Cave (about $50 million alleged, sold through YouTube ads), FBA Machine/Passive Scaling ($15.9 million), Ecommerce Empire Builders and Click Profit, several ending in permanent bans from selling business opportunities. Report suspected scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

So that’s the autopsy.

Somewhere back in my feed, the Silent Hill nurse is still twitching down her corridor with no face. And honestly? Hers is the more honest faceless business. She took twenty minutes, showed every step, and never once promised me ten grand. She only promised me nightmares. She delivered.

The cartoons are worth thirty-three cents per thousand. The platform has promised to stop paying for them. The kids can’t read the AI label. And somewhere a man is standing in a sandbox nobody has dug in, holding a golden shovel, under a ring light, explaining to a line of plastic shovels exactly how he got rich digging.

He didn’t dig.

He sold the shovel. You’re holding it.
