What Is a Honey Trap? Jensen Huang and History’s Spies

November 18, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

“Did You Know She Was a Spy?” A Sticky, Shameless History of the Honey Trap, From Delilah’s Scissors to Jensen Huang’s Wine Glass (and the One Question Nobody Thought to Ask)

There’s a sentence the President of the United States said on Monday, November 9, and I’ve been chewing on it for a week like a piece of gum I found under a bus seat in Constitución. It’s lost all its flavor. I can’t stop.

Here it is. Reporters asked Donald Trump about Jensen Huang, the leather-jacketed then-CEO of Nvidia, and the woman U.S. intelligence reportedly believes was a Chinese spy. And the President, with the calm of a man ordering a well-done steak, said he’d called Jensen up and asked him: “Did you have any idea that she was a spy?”

And Jensen said no. He had no idea.

“And I really believe him,” said the President.

Where was I when I heard it? Where every doomscrolling person on Earth is when the news finds them: on the can, phone in one hand, pants at half-mast, wondering what the f@#k my life had become. The clip autoplayed. I didn’t choose it. It chose me. The President of the United States, live, in my bathroom, at eye level with the toilet paper, explaining espionage to the press like a substitute teacher who skimmed the chapter in the parking lot. I didn’t flush. I couldn’t. I was in shock, and also mid-scroll, which is the same thing now.

I believe him too. Of course he didn’t know. That’s why she was a spy.

That is the entire job. That’s the whole résumé, the cover letter and the dental plan. Nobody in the four-thousand-year history of espionage has ever been honey-trapped by a woman who leaned across the bar, touched his forearm, and whispered, “Hey. Just so you know. I’m with the Ministry of State Security. Anyway, love the jacket.”

Asking a mark whether he knew is like asking a guy at the Miami Boat Show whether the shark mentioned it was a shark. Asking a tourist on the Florida subway… there’s no Florida subway. You see my point. It’s a question built so that the only possible answer is the one that clears everybody, which is either genius or a man who once suggested we nuke a hurricane. I genuinely can’t tell anymore.

Meanwhile, in my head, the two of them are already in my living room. One is doing “The Reynolds Pamphlet” from Hamilton, full ego trip, explaining the affair to the whole room like it’s a keynote with smoke machines. The other one is convinced Hamilton is a brand of ham and keeps cutting in with “My Bologna Has a First Name.” Don’t ask which one is which. Don’t ask why.

Also, a quick note for my own notes, because I had it wrong in my head too: what she allegedly did is not wet work. Wet work is spy slang for killing people. Wrong department. Honey traps are run out of the other wet department, the one with the scented candles and the hidden camera in the lampshade.

So with that said, let’s do what nobody in that press room did. Let’s talk about what a honey trap actually is, why it’s worked on everyone from Samson to a U.S. Marine guard in Moscow, and why the man who sold the shovels in the AI gold rush was the juiciest target on Earth.

First, the Story (Carefully, Because Lawyers Read Blogs Too)

Spy Story Fact On October 29, 2026, The New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence officials concluded Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le, no relation to Jensen) was a Chinese intelligence asset passing commercial information to Beijing. She was an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data-center company and Nvidia customer that had bought roughly $2 billion of Nvidia chips and was already under U.S. Commerce Department and Singapore police scrutiny over possible chip diversion to China. The Times reported that China’s Ministry of State Security recruited her to gather information on Jensen Huang and influence his feelings about selling AI chips to Chinese firms. On Sunday, November 8, Jensen Huang, 63, acknowledged an extramarital affair and apologized to his family. Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation and said it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. On Thursday, November 12, three days after the President said he believed him, Huang resigned as CEO; CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO, and Congress has summoned him to testify. No charges have been filed. The claims are anonymously sourced, and Nvidia and China dispute them.

Read that box twice. Then notice the line I’ll come back to: according to the reporting, U.S. officials don’t believe Jensen knew what she was.

Hold that thought. We’ll need it.

What Is a Honey Trap, for the People in the Back

A honey trap is an intelligence operation that uses romance or sex to get something out of a target: secrets, access, influence, or blackmail material, ideally all four with a side of fries.

Honey Trap Fact In Soviet intelligence slang, women trained to seduce targets were called “swallows” and men were “ravens.” A “swallow’s nest” was the apartment or hotel room, usually wired for sound and photographed through a two-way mirror. East Germany’s Stasi foreign-intelligence arm, run by spymaster Markus Wolf, made a specialty of male “Romeo” agents sent to court lonely secretaries in West German government offices.

The trap comes in three flavors:

The blackmail. Get the target into bed, get the photos, then show him the photos. This is the classic. It’s crude. It works because shame is a cheap, renewable resource.

The pipeline. The target doesn’t know, never knows, and just talks. Pillow talk, bar talk, “you won’t believe what happened at the office” talk. No photos needed. The lover is a microphone with perfume.

The nudge. The newest and most elegant. You don’t want his secrets. You want his opinions. You want a powerful man to wake up one morning feeling a little warmer about the thing you need him to feel warm about, and to believe the feeling was his idea. Remember this one. It’s the one the Times says Beijing was running.

A Short, Sticky History of the Honey Trap

Here’s the thing about honey traps. They’re older than writing. They’re older than money. They’re probably older than lying, which they require. Somewhere in a cave forty thousand years ago, a guy was offered the good piece of mammoth by a very attentive cavewoman from the next valley over, and three weeks later his whole tribe’s favorite hunting spot had mysteriously been “discovered” by the next valley over.

And what was the cave bro thinking while he chewed? Not about the hunting spot. Too much gristle. Then: Really should do something about the lice down there. Animal pelts in that region were a bad idea. And for one shimmering second he peered forty thousand years into the future and saw Ross from Friends, alone in a bathroom, losing a war with a pair of leather pants. He shuddered. He took another bite. The next valley over took his hunting spot.

Delilah, roughly 1100 B.C., give or take a prophet. In the Book of Judges, the Philistine lords each offer Delilah eleven hundred pieces of silver to find out where Samson gets his strength. That’s not a bribe. That’s a syndicated loan. She asks him. He lies. She ties him up and yells, “The Philistines are upon you, Samson!” He snaps the ropes. She asks again. He lies again. She does it again. Three times she runs the play right in front of him, out loud, with the enemy hiding in the next room, and the fourth time he tells her the truth about the hair. Pin that. It’s the ending.

Xi Shi, around 490 B.C. During the Spring and Autumn period, King Goujian of Yue, freshly humiliated by King Fuchai of Wu, allegedly sent Fuchai the most beautiful woman in the kingdom as a gift. Fuchai fell so hard he stopped governing, started building palaces, and generally treated statecraft like a gym membership. Yue conquered Wu in 473 B.C. Xi Shi became one of the Four Beauties of ancient China. Fuchai became a cautionary tale and probably a meme carved on a turtle shell.

Stratagem Fact China later codified the move. The Thirty-Six Stratagems, a collection of classic Chinese tactics, lists Měi rén jì, “the beauty trap,” as stratagem number 31. It sits in the section called “stratagems for desperate situations.” Which tells you exactly how the ancients felt about using it, and exactly how often they did anyway.

So when someone says the honey trap is a Cold War thing, a James Bond thing, a Russian thing, gently remind them that it has a chapter number in a Chinese playbook older than the printing press.

Mata Hari, 1917. The most famous seductress-spy in history, and quite possibly not much of a spy at all. Margaretha Zelle, a Dutch exotic dancer who reinvented herself in Paris as a Javanese temple goddess (she was from Leeuwarden, which is a little like a guy from Ohio reinventing himself as a Tibetan lama), took lovers from every army in Europe during World War I. The French shot her by firing squad on October 15, 1917, as a German spy. Historians have argued for a century that the evidence was thin and that France needed a villain more than it needed a verdict. The legend is the honey trap. The reality might have been a scapegoat in a feathered headdress.

John Vassall, 1954. A clerk at the British Embassy in Moscow, gay at a time when that was illegal back home, was invited to a party, plied with drinks, and photographed with men. The KGB showed him the pictures. He handed over Admiralty secrets for years and was finally caught and sentenced in 1962 to eighteen years. That’s the blackmail flavor at its cruelest. They didn’t exploit his desire. They exploited his country’s laws.

Clayton Lonetree, 1986. A young U.S. Marine guarding the American Embassy in Moscow fell for a Soviet woman named Violetta Seina, who worked as a translator at the embassy. She introduced him to her “Uncle Sasha.” Uncle Sasha was KGB. Lonetree became the first Marine convicted of espionage and was sentenced in 1987 to thirty years, later reduced. Somewhere in the Marine Corps there’s a slide in a PowerPoint that just says “UNCLE SASHA” in red and nobody laughs.

Mordechai Vanunu, 1986. The reverse direction, and proof everybody does it. Vanunu, a former technician at Israel’s Dimona nuclear facility, was in London spilling Israel’s nuclear secrets to The Sunday Times. A woman calling herself “Cindy” befriended him and suggested a romantic weekend in Rome. In Rome, Mossad agents grabbed him, drugged him and shipped him to Israel, where he served eighteen years. “Cindy” was a Mossad officer. Vanunu thought he was getting a gelato and a girlfriend. He got a hood and a cargo hold.

Argentina, for the record, has its own spy agency, born in 1946 as the SIDE, renamed the SI, rebuilt as the AFI, then turned back into the SIDE in 2024 by a president who said the AFI had been used for internal espionage and political persecution, which means our spooks have changed names more times than a telenovela villain with amnesia.

Notice the pattern? Not one of these men knew. Not Fuchai, not Vassall, not the Marine. That’s not a bug of the honey trap. That’s the entire product.

STOP.

Read the President’s Sentence Again.

Pull the car over. Hazards on. Let’s look at the thing that’s been stuck in my teeth.

“Did you have any idea that she was a spy?” “No.” “I really believe him.”

As a defense, it’s useless. It’s like the security guard at the Louvre saying he asked the guy carrying the Mona Lisa out the side door whether he was stealing it, and the guy said no, and honestly, he seemed nice.

But here’s where it gets weird, and why I owe the man a grudging, sticky little nod.

According to the reporting, U.S. officials don’t believe Jensen knew either. The President, by accident, in the most backward way possible, said the one thing the spooks reportedly agree on. A stopped clock in a gold frame, right twice a day, loudly, on camera.

The problem was never whether Jensen knew. The problem is that not knowing is exactly what a working honey trap looks like from the inside. The President asked the one question whose answer can’t tell you anything. The useful question is the one nobody asked out loud: what did she want him to feel, and did he feel it?

Why Jensen? Follow the Chips

Here’s the AI part, and why this isn’t just tabloid fodder.

Nvidia makes the graphics processors that train and run most of the world’s big AI models. If AI is the gold rush, Jensen Huang wasn’t a prospector. Until last Thursday, he was the guy who owned every shovel store between here and the river, and he was the one who decided which countries got to buy shovels at all, along with a very short list of officials in Washington.

The U.S. has spent years restricting which Nvidia chips can be sold to China. Megaspeed, per the Times, bought billions of dollars’ worth and was already under investigation for possibly routing them where they weren’t supposed to go. Nvidia says those allegations were investigated and that nothing was compromised.

Now go back to the three flavors. Nobody needed to steal Nvidia’s blueprints. The Times says the alleged goal was the third flavor, the nudge. Warm a man’s feelings toward selling chips to China. Shift his mood about export policy by a degree or two. Let him carry that mood into rooms where policy gets made.

That’s the 2026 honey trap. In an industry where the secrets live in a server farm guarded like Fort Knox, the softest attack surface isn’t the firewall. It’s the mood of the man who has the President’s phone number.

Delilah wanted the source of his strength. Beijing, allegedly, wanted the source of his opinions. Same play. Bigger stadium.

Self-Rebuttal: You’re Building a Spy Novel Out of Anonymous Sources

Fair. Let me knock this down before the top comment does.

It’s an allegation, not a verdict. The core claim comes from unnamed people briefed on U.S. surveillance. No one has been charged. Nvidia disputes it. China disputes it. And since then, CNN has reported that U.S. officials themselves are divided on whether the evidence shows she was a spy at all; the Justice Department says it has reached no conclusion. Intelligence assessments have been wrong before, sometimes spectacularly, sometimes with a war attached.

”Honey trap” can be a lazy frame. It turns the woman into a weapon and the man into a victim of his own glands, and it’s suspiciously convenient for powerful men who’d rather be targets than adulterers. Credit where due: Jensen’s own statement didn’t hide behind it. He called the affair his failing and apologized to his wife and kids. Whatever she was, his marriage was his to keep.

And maybe nothing happened. Nvidia’s board says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised. It’s entirely possible this is a story about a man having an affair with someone his government was already watching, and the “nudge” never moved anything.

All true. None of it changes the point. The honey trap is the cheapest, oldest, most reliable intelligence tool in history because it targets the one system no company can patch: a person who feels understood. If you’re running the most strategically important company on Earth, the question isn’t “Did you know?” It’s “Who’s paid to make you feel this way?”

How to Tell If You’re Being Honey-Trapped (A Field Guide for Billionaires and Embassy Marines)

1. You are not that charming. If someone half your age finds your thoughts on semiconductor export licensing fascinating, consult a mirror before you consult your heart.

2. Watch what they want you to feel, not what they ask you to give. The pros never ask for the file. They ask how you feel about the file.

3. Meet Uncle Sasha in daylight. Any new lover with an uncle who wants to talk shop is not a lover. It’s a recruitment pitch with dinner.

4. Romantic weekend in Rome? Ask Vanunu.

Oh crap. From the rearview, I’m looking at what I just did, two thousand words dunking on the leader of the free world for asking a dumb question, and I’m wondering if I’m the one who got played. Maybe the dumb question was the honey. Maybe I’m the fly, stuck in it, typing on the can.

Then I remember the boat show. Somewhere in Miami, right now, a shark is circling a forty-foot yacht with a bar on the flybridge, and not one person on that deck is asking it whether it’s a shark. It never said it was. It didn’t have to. The fin did the talking.

Neither did she.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

What is a honey trap?

A honey trap is an intelligence operation that uses romantic or sexual relationships to gain secrets, access, influence or blackmail material from a target. The target usually doesn’t know the relationship is part of an operation.

What are some famous honey traps in history?

Well-known cases include Delilah and Samson in the Book of Judges, Xi Shi in ancient China, British clerk John Vassall in 1950s Moscow, U.S. Marine Clayton Lonetree at the Moscow embassy in the 1980s, and Mossad’s 1986 luring of Mordechai Vanunu to Rome by an agent called “Cindy.”

What are “swallows” and “ravens”?

Soviet intelligence slang for operatives trained to seduce targets: swallows were women, ravens were men. The rooms where traps were set were called swallows’ nests.

Is the honey trap in the Thirty-Six Stratagems?

Yes. “The beauty trap” (Měi rén jì) is stratagem number 31 in the classic Chinese collection of Thirty-Six Stratagems.

What is the Jensen Huang spy story?

The New York Times reported in October 2026 that U.S. intelligence officials believe Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, was a Chinese intelligence asset close to then-Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. He acknowledged an affair and resigned as CEO on November 12, 2026. No charges have been filed, U.S. officials are reportedly divided on the espionage claims, and Nvidia and China dispute them.

What did Trump say about Jensen Huang and the spy?

On November 9, 2026, Trump said he asked Huang whether he had any idea she was a spy, that Huang said he had no idea, and that he believed him.

Was Mata Hari really a spy?

France executed her as a German spy in 1917, but many historians argue the evidence against her was weak and that she may have been more scapegoat than spymaster.

So. Back to Samson, because I promised.

Delilah didn’t hide what she was. She asked him three times, out loud. Three times she tied him up and shouted that the enemy was coming, and the enemy was coming, from the next room. And the fourth time, he told her anyway.

Which means the President’s question was wrong in both directions. Not knowing doesn’t prove you’re clean. And knowing doesn’t save you. The oldest honey trap on record told the mark exactly what it was, three times, and he still handed over the scissors.
