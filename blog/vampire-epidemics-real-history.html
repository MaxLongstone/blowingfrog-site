# The Dead Weren’t Spreading Anything but Rumors: A Field Guide to the Vampire Epidemics of Yorkshire, Serbia, Moravia and Rhode Island

Close your eyes and picture a vampire. Go ahead, I’ll wait.

Cape. Cheekbones. An accent from nowhere, a skincare routine that costs more than your car, and a deep, fatal interest in your neck. Bela Lugosi by way of Count Chocula, approved by every teenager who ever sat through a Twilight sequel.

Now open your eyes, because you’re wrong. Lovely person, terrible guesser.

The first vampires the Western world took seriously were puffy, ruddy Serbian farmers, weeks in the dirt, fresh blood at the corners of the mouth, looking like Jack Black after the all-you-can-eat crab leg night. And the people who certified them weren’t peasants with pitchforks. They were army surgeons. With a commission. And a signed report.

Confession: vampires never scared me. Not once. Somewhere in the middle of Interview with the Vampire, while most of my generation was hiding behind a pillow, I was the weirdo who wanted to be Lestat. Wanted to BE him. Which, if you’ve seen What We Do in the Shadows, makes me Guillermo: the familiar who’ll run the vampires’ errands for years on the off chance somebody finally bites him. Yes. I’ve made peace with it. Mostly.

This is the history of the vampire epidemics. Historians call the Habsburg chapter the 18th-century vampire controversy. The internet calls it the Great Vampire Epidemic. Either way, it started in a Yorkshire monastery, took a hard left into Habsburg Serbia, got shut down by an Empress holding a pen, and then, I swear to God, turned up in Rhode Island in 1892… a full decade after a German doctor named Robert Koch had found the bacterium behind tuberculosis.

Pour something brown. We’re digging.

What Were the Vampire Epidemics?

A vampire epidemic is a cluster of deaths in one community that the survivors blamed on a recently buried neighbor, followed by an exhumation, a stake, a fire and, in the good cases, paperwork. They hit medieval England, a Greek island in 1701, Habsburg Serbia in the 1720s and 1730s, Moravia in the 1750s, and rural New England right up to 1892.

The common thread isn’t Transylvania. It isn’t capes. It’s a disease nobody could explain and a body nobody could stop looking at.

Medieval Vampires: A Yorkshire Monk With a Ghost Problem

Around 1198, an Augustinian canon named William of Newburgh sat down in Yorkshire to finish a history of England from 1066 onward. He prided himself on reliable sources. Sober fellow. Not a man you’d expect to go sideways.

Then, in Book V, between the kings and the crusades, he stops the whole sh!tshow to tell you about the dead walking around Britain.

A man in Buckinghamshire dies and keeps coming home to his widow. A bishop gets involved, and the husband stays in the ground. That’s medieval marriage counseling. In Berwick and Alnwick the dead walk and a plague follows, William ties the two together, and the townsfolk answer the way townsfolk always answered… with a bonfire.

Medieval public health policy, in one sentence: when in doubt, light it.

The word “vampire” wouldn’t reach English for more than five hundred years. William never uses it. His revenants don’t need it. They’re already doing the job: dead, local, blamed for the sick.

Scholars who date the “classical” European vampire put its run at about sixty years, from a 1672 case in Istria (a man named Jure Grando, in what’s now Croatia) to the Serbian outbreak of 1731 and 1732. William’s revenants are the prequel. The word wasn’t invented yet. The plot was already in the can.

A Quick Stop on Mykonos, 1701, Where the Vampire Drank Everything Except Blood

Long before Mykonos became a place to lose your sandals and your dignity, a French botanist named Joseph Pitton de Tournefort wandered in and watched an island lose its mind over a dead man who wouldn’t stay put.

The locals said the guy was out at night breaking doors, tearing clothes and, worst of all, emptying every bottle in the house. Tournefort decided he was the thirstiest devil in the Aegean. The town said a Mass and put the butcher, a clumsy old fellow, in charge of cutting out the heart. The smell was so foul that Tournefort noted the crowd insisted it didn’t smell. They burned the heart on the shore. Didn’t work. Then, on New Year’s Day, they burned the whole corpse in what the botanist called a bonfire of joy. The haunting stopped.

Notice what’s missing. Not one drop of blood drinking. Early vampires didn’t want your blood. They wanted your wine, your sleep and your peace and quiet. Which, frankly, makes them a roommate.

Kisiljevo, 1725: Nine Dead in Two Days and One Convenient Neighbor

Now we get to Serbia, and it’s about to get paper-heavy.

Petar Blagojević was a peasant in Kisiljevo, a Danube village in the slice of Serbia the Habsburgs had grabbed from the Ottomans in 1718. He died in 1725 and was buried with the usual church ceremony. The Austrian clerks wrote his name Peter Plogojowitz, because Austrian clerks spelled Serbian names the way you spell your ex’s name in a drunk text.

Within a short time, nine villagers died over two days. Each of them said, before going, that Petar had visited in the night and strangled them.

Nobody in Kisiljevo said, “Nine in two days, huh, maybe check the well.” They said, “Check Petar.”

The neighbors wanted him dug up. A Habsburg official arrived to find the grave already open and was astonished at what he saw: a body that, by local standards, had every vampire credential. Blood showed at the mouth and nose when the stake went in. They burned him. The deaths stopped being reported. An official report, with the word vampir in it, went up the chain.

Two hundred kilometers away, another village was about to do the same thing, and this time the whole continent was going to read about it.

Medveđa, 1726 to 1732: The Report That Went Viral Before Viral Existed

Here’s where it gets good, and by good I mean you couldn’t make this up if you were paid by the word.

Arnold Paole, a Serbian militiaman and frontier hajduk, tells his neighbors he was plagued by a vampire down in Ottoman territory. His cure: eat dirt from the vampire’s grave and smear himself with its blood. That’s the worst smoothie in recorded history. Then, around 1726, he fell off a hay wagon and broke his neck.

The universe hates a smartass with a hay wagon.

Twenty or thirty days later, four villagers complained that Paole was visiting them at night. All four died. Forty days after the burial, the neighbors dug him up. The report says fresh blood had run from his eyes, ears and nose, and the old nails and skin had fallen off, with new ones underneath. They drove a stake through his heart. He groaned. He bled. They cut off his head and burned him.

And that was the warm-up act.

Five years later it starts again. By January 1732, seventeen people had died in three months, young, healthy ones among them. The symptoms: stabbing pains in the side, chest pain, a long fever, limbs that jerked. The village blamed two women, Milica and Stana. And they blamed the meat. Milica had eaten sheep that Paole’s crew, the “previous vampires,” had killed. That was the villagers’ theory of transmission: you are what you ate, and what you ate was killed by a ghost.

The local authorities, fearing plague, called in an imperial contagion doctor, Martin Glaser, who examined the village in December of 1731. Then the army sent a bigger commission, led by a regimental surgeon named Johannes Flückinger, who arrived on January 7, 1732. They dug. They cut. They wrote. More than a dozen of the bodies came out “vampires” by the standards of the day (the sources split between thirteen and sixteen, and I’m not freaking Sherlock Holmes, I can’t settle it).

The report, Visum et Repertum, “Seen and Discovered,” was published in Nuremberg that same year and ran through Europe like a wet match in a hay barn. By 1734 the word “vampire” had landed in English.

Read that again. Every vampire movie you’ve ever watched is fan fiction of an army medical filing. Dracula, Lestat, Edward Cullen, every sad guy in a velvet jacket on What We Do in the Shadows… all of them descended from a regimental surgeon’s homework. Guillermo included, God bless him.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau looked at the case file and complained that the evidence for vampires was perfect: official reports, affidavits from surgeons, priests and magistrates, the whole judicial package. And, he added, nobody believed in them. He had a point. He just didn’t have a better explanation.
War of the Worlds Fact On October 30, 1938, the night before Halloween, Orson Welles and the Mercury Theatre on the Air sent a Martian invasion out over CBS, written as a string of news bulletins. It opened with a disclaimer that it was a story. The next morning the papers screamed about mass panic, and the standard accounts say those papers inflated it. Different century, same engine: something that sounds like a news report, and a public that fills in the rest. One difference. Welles told you it was fiction. The Austrian army filed theirs as fact.

What the Surgeons Actually Saw (Spoiler: Death Has Terrible PR)

Here’s the part that makes grown men put down their beer.

Almost everything the surgeons reported is what a dead body does. The folklorist Paul Barber spent years on this and concluded that vampire lore is an elaborate folk hypothesis, a way to explain perfectly natural things that happen to the dead. Bodies swell, which makes the corpse look plump and well fed. Blood and fluid can show at the mouth and nose. Skin and nails slough off, and if you’ve never seen it, a layer of fresh-looking skin underneath reads as “new.” Cold weather slows the whole show down. And a body can make sounds when you drive a stake into it, because it’s a bag of air and liquid, and the stake is not gentle.

I’m not freaking Quincy, M.E., so go read Barber, but the gist is this: the villagers weren’t hallucinating. They were looking at normal decay and drawing the wrong conclusion from it.

One historian described the Serbian vampire as a bloated skin bag, blown up and full of blood. Not a seducer. A wineskin in a nightshirt. Hollywood did a lot of work on this guy.

There’s a second layer, and it’s harder to laugh at. A Spanish neurologist named Juan Gómez-Alonso proposed in 1998 that rabies could explain some of it: the nocturnal agitation, the biting, the apparent death and return. Other researchers have floated other diseases for other outbreaks. Nothing fits every case, and nobody will ever prove which, because the evidence is a pile of graves that were burned.

STOP.

Pull the car over. Hazards on. Get out of the car.

You’ve been nodding along thinking this is a Balkan peasants thing. Those people. Back then. Over there. It’s the intellectual equivalent of a bumper sticker that says COEXIST on a truck that’s tailgating a minivan.

Hold on, because I’m about to jump the median.

You thought I was going left and I’m taking the whole car across the Atlantic like the Duke boys over a creek, horn blaring… Next stop: Rhode Island. In the nineteenth century. After the Civil War. After the telegraph. After germ theory.

Rhode Island, 1892: The Dead Don’t Care What Century It Is

In the 1800s, tuberculosis, which people called consumption, tore through New England families. It took the mother, then the oldest daughter, then the next kid, and nobody could say why it hopped from one to another. So the folk explanation went: the first to die is draining the others. Dig the dead up. If the body looks wrong, burn the heart, sometimes the liver, and have the sick relative drink the ashes in water.

A researcher named Diana Ross McCain counted eighteen documented exhumations like this across New England in the 18th and 19th centuries. Thoreau mentioned one in his 1859 journal. This wasn’t a fringe thing. It was a regional medical practice.

I’m going to put the jokes down for a minute. Mercy Brown was nineteen.

She lived on a farm in Exeter, Rhode Island. George Brown, her father, had already buried his wife, Mary Eliza, and a daughter, Mary Olive. His son Edwin was sick. Mercy got sick and died on January 19, 1892. The neighbors wanted the bodies dug up, and George, who didn’t believe the theory, gave a reluctant yes.

On March 17, 1892, at Chestnut Hill Cemetery, they opened three graves. The mother and the sister, ten years in the ground, were bones. Mercy, two months dead in a Rhode Island winter, looked intact, and there was blood still in her heart. A doctor was there and said it was consumption. The neighbors didn’t buy it. They cut out her heart, burned it on a nearby rock, mixed the ashes into water, and Edwin drank it.

He died on May 2, 1892.

That was ten years after Robert Koch announced he’d identified the bacterium that causes tuberculosis. The cure was already on the way. The ritual just got there first.

The Empress Who Banned the Undead

Back in Europe, the panic had a very specific, very final ending, and it came from a woman with a pen.

In 1755, in a village called Hermersdorf in Moravia, a corpse was pulled out through a hole in the cemetery wall with a hook on a rope, beheaded and burned outside. When Empress Maria Theresa heard, she sent two court doctors to look. Then she asked her personal physician, a Dutchman named Gerard van Swieten, what was going on.

Van Swieten concluded that the “vampires” were produced by epidemic illness with fevers and hallucinations. On March 1, 1755, the Empress issued a decree: no more desecrating graves in the name of vampire defense, and reports of vampirism went under censorship. She lumped vampires in with witch-hunting, fortune-telling and digging for treasure. A woman who had given birth to sixteen children was not about to be bullied by a corpse.

There’s a long-running theory that van Swieten was the model for Van Helsing in Dracula. It’s plausible, it’s a great story, and I’d bet my last Alfajor it’s mostly vibes. Treat it as a legend with a nice haircut.

The Self-Rebuttal: Maybe the Villagers Had a Point

Before you pat yourself on the head, here’s the argument against the smug version of this post.

First, “it was just decomposition” is too neat. People were dying. Nine in two days in Kisiljevo. Seventeen in three months in Medveđa. Authorities called in a contagion doctor because they feared plague. The vampire wasn’t wrong that something was spreading. It was wrong about what.

Second, the villagers weren’t hysterical dopes. They had a hypothesis (the dead are draining the living), a test (open the grave and look), and a treatment (stake, fire, ashes). The protocol failed because the hypothesis was wrong, but the shape of it is recognizable. It’s what humans do when they can’t see the germ.

Third, the famous version of Mercy Brown is thinner than the legend. Historians who went back to the Exeter paper trail found that much of what we “know” about her was written after the fact, and the details are slim. I told her story straight. I didn’t tell it with a bow on top.

Fourth, rabies, bad meat, pellagra, tuberculosis: each fits one case and breaks on the next. I’m not a pathologist. Nobody gets to say “case closed” on a pile of burned graves.

Fifth, the panic itself might be a story. The famous 1938 radio panic was inflated by the newspapers that reported it, and I’m not freaking a sociologist, so assume some of what reached us about the vampire panics was inflated on the way, too. The deaths were real. How scared everybody was is the one thing I can’t measure.

Somewhere there’s a churchyard, a lantern, and a stake driven clean through a stack of paperwork, a red wax seal dripping off the top like the world’s most bureaucratic crime scene.

That’s how the vampires actually died, by the way. Not with garlic. Not with a crucifix. With a decree, a better explanation and a stamp.

Sleep tight.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Were vampire epidemics real?

The deaths were real. The vampires weren’t. Communities in medieval England, 18th-century Serbia and Moravia, and 19th-century New England suffered clusters of illness and blamed a recently dead neighbor.

Where did the vampire panic start?

The best-documented outbreaks are in Habsburg Serbia, at Kisiljevo in 1725 and Medveđa in 1731 and 1732, where Austrian army surgeons wrote the reports that carried the word “vampire” across Europe.

Who was Arnold Paole?

A Serbian militiaman who said he’d cured a vampire bite with grave dirt and blood, died around 1726 in a fall from a hay wagon, and was dug up forty days later by neighbors who decided he was a vampire.

Who was the last vampire accused in America?

Mercy Brown of Exeter, Rhode Island, exhumed in March 1892, is the best-documented and last widely known New England case.

What really causes vampire “signs” in a corpse?

Natural decomposition: bloating, fluid at the mouth and nose, sloughing skin and nails. Disease explanations like rabies have also been proposed for some outbreaks, though none fits every case.

Did anyone stop the vampire hunts?

Yes. In 1755, Empress Maria Theresa banned the desecration of graves in the name of vampire defense, after her physician Gerard van Swieten investigated.
