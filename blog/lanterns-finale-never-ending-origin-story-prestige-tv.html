Lanterns and the Never-Ending Origin Story Problem

October 31, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

Eight Hours to Put On a Ring: Prestige TV Has Discovered Edging, and I’d Like to Speak to the Manager of the Multiverse

Episode two. Last minute.

A man in a green suit leaves the planet, flies to a space prison, and walks up to a cell where a bald, gaunt, magnificently disappointed alien is waiting for him like a landlord who already knows about the dog.

Sinestro.

I sat up. Not a figure of speech. My spine left the couch cushion of its own accord, the way a meerkat’s does when it hears a hawk, and I might have moaned, in the same way my wife has heard me when it’s rowdy time… and put on a goofy face of absolute pleasure. YES!

That was HBO’s Lanterns, episode two, “Trust Fall,” and for about forty-eight hours afterward the internet behaved like a frat house that had found a wizard’s spellbook in the basement. Yellow light meant Parallax. Somebody’s cousin’s roommate was SURE the Blackest Night was coming and Nekron was already in the walls. Every secondary character with a slightly flushed complexion was getting accused of being a Red Lantern.

And yes, you. I’m looking at you. You had a Google Doc. You had color-coded tabs. Don’t lie to me, I can see the tabs from here.

The show premiered on August 16, it pulled 9.3 million viewers worldwide in its first three days, and the breadcrumbs were everywhere. Cosmic stuff was COMING. You could smell it, like ozone before a storm, or like a Cinnabon in an airport, which is the same feeling but with more regret.

Then, slowly, over eight Sundays, I got the nagging feeling.

You know the one. It’s the feeling you get when a first date keeps describing the restaurant they’re going to take you to “next time.” It’s a promissory note with a nice haircut.

John Stewart. With an H. And a Ring in His Pocket.

Quick roll call for the people in the back who are only here because the title said “edging.”

Lanterns follows two Green Lanterns: Hal Jordan, the veteran, played by Kyle Chandler with the dry, sun-cured weariness of a man who has coached high school football and the universe in the same week, and John Stewart, the recruit, played by Aaron Pierre. Not the Daily Show guy. The one with the H. The one who, in the comics, is an architect and a Marine and one of the great Green Lanterns of all time.

The show is a murder mystery in Rushville, Nebraska, a True Detective riff with space cops, co-created by Chris Mundy, Damon Lindelof and Tom King), with Mundy as showrunner, Lindelof writing the pilot, and the whole thing running under James Gunn and Peter Safran’s DC Studios.

And Lindelof, to my eye, was digging through his own record collection. His Watchmen for HBO won eleven Emmys in 2020, and it was also a legacy-hero story wrapped around a small-town mystery wrapped around a secret identity wrapped around a question about who gets to wear the mask. Lanterns is that, with a ring. Greatest hits, remastered, new cover art, same B-side.

That’s not an insult, by the way. If I had eleven Emmys I’d play them at every party too. I’d play them at funerals.

Here’s the timeline that matters:

Episode two: Sinestro, in a cell, warning Hal that the greater threat is John.

Episode five: John finally gets Hal’s ring in his hand.

Episode eight, the finale, “Dirt and Stars,” October 4: John finally accepts the role and speaks his own custom version of the oath.

He had it in episode five. He put it on in episode eight.

Three episodes. That’s three hours of prestige television in which a man walks around with the thing on the poster in his PANTS.

And even then, co-creator Tom King explained after the finale that John is “not a hundred percent a Green Lantern anymore.” Guy Gardner still has the official ring for the sector. So we waited eight hours for the ring, got the ring, and were told the ring comes with an asterisk.

Before the season was half done, showrunner Chris Mundy had also explained why Sinestro wasn’t going to get his villainous due this season. Which is a sentence I’d like tattooed on the inside of every network executive’s eyelids: the villain on the poster isn’t going to do anything this year.

The critics adored it. One reviewer said it pulled off the rare feat of telling a complete story. The finale did 10.5 million viewers globally, a record for the show. People called it a complete synthesis of the season. It was, honest to God, awe-inspiring.

And it left a taste in my throat like I’d licked the inside of a jar of pennies.

Because I’ve SEEN this. I’ve seen it so many times I could do it in my sleep, and I think I have, and I think in the dream I was also wearing a cape I hadn’t earned yet.

Groundhog Day, Sponsored by a Ring

Smallville ran for ten seasons. Ten. Two hundred and seventeen episodes of a young man in a flannel shirt NOT flying. The deal was right there in the pitch: no tights, no flights. And they honored it, God help them, for a decade, until the series finale finally let Clark get airborne and showed us the suit in glimpses so brief you could’ve missed them by blinking or by having a child.

The audience aged in real time. People went from high school to mortgages waiting for that boy to leave the ground. Somebody started watching Smallville single and finished it divorced.

Now look at Frank Castle, because the Punisher is the cleanest case study in the whole genre, a man stuck in a revolving door that someone keeps spinning with a cattle prod.

2016: Daredevil season two introduces Jon Bernthal’s Punisher. Origin story.

2017: The Punisher season one. Origin story, again, deeper.

2019: The Punisher season two. By my count, back to square one, a man figuring out who he is.

May 12, 2026: The Punisher: One Last Kill, a one-hour Disney+ special co-written by Bernthal. And in the climactic final scene, Frank puts his skull vest back on.

The final scene. Of a one-hour special. About the Punisher. Becoming the Punisher.

I’ll be fair to Frank. His history is a crime scene of licensing deals, Netflix contracts, a cancellation, a studio handoff and enough lawyers to staff a small Baltic nation. Some of that loop is paperwork, not storytelling. Same partial pardon for Daredevil.

But that’s an explanation, not a defense. And it is the exact same feeling as watching Bill Murray’s clock radio flip to six a.m. in Groundhog Day, hearing “I Got You Babe” start up for the four-hundredth time, and realizing that somewhere above you a writers’ room has decided Punxsutawney is the whole show.

Origin Fact In Walter Mischel’s famous delayed-gratification experiments at Stanford’s Bing Nursery School in the late 1960s and early ’70s, preschoolers were offered one treat now or two if they could wait, typically up to about fifteen minutes. Fifteen minutes. Clark Kent took ten years.

STOP.

Pull over. Hazards on. Get out of the car.

We’re about to cross the street into the Marvel neighborhood, and some of you are already reaching for the comment box with a Funko Pop of a Marvel character staring at you from the shelf like a disappointed Catholic saint. Put the Funko down. I see it. I see the lanyard from a convention you went to in 2019 that you still wear to the supermarket. We’re doing this anyway.

Meanwhile, Across the Street, Somebody Just Kicks the Door In

I know. I KNOW. Marvel’s Disney+ shows catch a ton of flak, and some of it is earned, and I’m not freaking Kevin Feige, so I won’t pretend to know which budget line paid for which bad green screen.

But look at how fast they get to the thing.

Loki, season one, opens on a mystery: someone is killing Time Variance Authority agents. By the end of episode two, we know who: another Loki. Mystery answered in the SECOND HOUR, and then the show spends the rest of its run going bonkers with what the answer means.

The Falcon and the Winter Soldier opens with an aerial chase. Not a conversation about whether Sam might someday enjoy flying. A man in wings, in the air, punching.

Moon Knight. By the end of episode one, Steven Grant, a mild gift-shop employee at a London museum with a goldfish and a sleep disorder, has stopped being mild. He hands the wheel to Marc, the suit arrives, and a jackal monster gets its face rearranged inside the museum. Episode ONE.

WandaVision plays sitcom dress-up, but by the end of episode three the mask slips and you understand Wanda is the one holding the remote. That’s the reveal most shows would save for the finale. They spent it in week three like a sailor on shore leave.

Agatha All Along gets the Salem Seven riding out of the fog inside its two-episode premiere.

And now VisionQuest, which premiered October 14. I went in thinking it would be one beautiful, harebrained idea, AIs fighting it out inside Vision’s mind, a philosophy seminar with laser effects. Instead it’s an action show with a pulse. James Spader’s Ultron is central from the get-go, and Jocasta, T’Nia Miller, made her debut in episodes three and four on October 20. Episode three. I have no idea where it’s going, and that’s the THRILL of it, because you can feel the proportion: it isn’t hoarding its toys. It’s playing with them in front of you, and occasionally setting them on fire.

You can disagree with me about whether any of those shows is GOOD. I’ll argue the other side with you at a bar for three hours, I’ll lose, I’ll pay. But they cash the check. They put the thing on the poster in the first act and then dare themselves to top it.

The DC and HBO approach is the opposite. It’s a magician who spends forty-five minutes showing you the box is empty, rapping on its sides, inviting a volunteer up to check the hinges, and then the credits roll and an announcer says the saw comes out next season. If renewed.

The Marshmallow Has Grown a Beard

Back to the nursery school, because it explains EVERYTHING.

In Mischel’s experiments, a kid sits at a table with one treat. The researcher says: eat it now, or wait, and you’ll get two. Then the researcher leaves the room. Some kids eat it in a heartbeat. Some sing to themselves, cover their eyes, kick the table, stare at the ceiling like monks, and hold out.

The ones who held out got TWO.

That’s the deal. That’s the entire social contract of delayed gratification: you suffer now, and the universe pays you back with interest. Even the follow-up science respects the deal. A 2018 replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan found the long-term link between waiting and later success was much weaker than the famous version, and largely explained by kids’ backgrounds. The marshmallow isn’t destiny. But the kid who waited still got the second marshmallow. Nobody walked back into the room and said, “Great job waiting. Here’s the same one marshmallow, now wearing a ring, and also we’ve renewed you for another fifteen minutes.”

That’s the never-ending origin story. The marshmallow sits there so long it grows a beard. The beard turns gray. The timer rusts. The kid is in college. The marshmallow becomes a Gandalf and starts giving everyone advice about the wait being the point.

And here’s my theory, which is a theory, from a guy with a blog and an HBO Max password, not a leaked memo.

An origin story is a subscription with a plot.

Lanterns premiered August 16 and ended October 4. That’s forty-nine days. Two billing cycles. Weekly drops of a story whose payoff lives in the last episode are a beautiful machine for keeping your card on file through both of them. A show that gives you the ring in episode two gives you permission to leave. A show that gives you the ring in episode eight keeps you paying to find out if the ring exists.

So the wait isn’t a side effect of the storytelling. Increasingly, the wait IS the product. It’s the thing being sold, measured, renewed. Television has discovered edging, and it’s billing us monthly for the privilege.

I’m not Geoff Johns. I can’t tell you which color of the emotional spectrum corresponds to “mild abdominal tension during a recap episode.” But I’d bet you a power battery it’s yellow, and it’s FEAR. Fear you’ll cancel before the good part.

Lost Built the Box. Everybody Else Bought the Franchise.

None of this started at HBO. It started on an island.

Lost ran from 2004 to 2010, co-created by J.J. Abrams, Damon Lindelof and Jeffrey Lieber. In 2007, Abrams gave a TED talk about the “mystery box,” a literal magic box he bought as a kid and never opened, because the not-knowing was more exciting than whatever was inside. That talk became gospel. Mystery is better than knowledge. The question is the product. The box stays shut.

And look who’s back, on a show built around a box (a ring box, really) that stays shut for eight hours. Lindelof’s career is a man walking the same box from network to network like a dog that won’t stop bringing you the same slobbered tennis ball. To be clear, it’s a very GOOD tennis ball. The Leftovers is one of the best things ever made for television. But the dog has a type.

The trick worked so well that it became a trope, and the trope became a business model, and the business model became the weather. Amazon’s The Rings of Power spent its entire first season on the question “who is Sauron?” and answered it in the season finale, eight episodes later, like a waiter who brings you the bread basket and then the check. Netflix has its own hall of shame, and I’ll let you fill it in below, because you have opinions and I have a word count.

We’ve started accepting it. That’s the part that bugs me. We’ve stopped asking for the payoff and started thanking them for the foreplay. We grade the breadcrumbs. We review the ANTICIPATION. Somewhere a critic is writing a five-star review of a trailer for a trailer.

Wow Factor Is a Payment Schedule

Here’s the case that made me want to write all of this.

House of the Dragon. (I had to go look up the name. It’s the one with the Targaryens and the dragons and the wigs that look like they’re shedding their winter coats. The Game of Thrones prequel.)

Season one: fantastic. It ends on a cliffhanger, a dead son and a mother’s face that says the war is now inevitable. We’re going to war. Finally.

Season two: when is the war coming? When is the war coming? When IS the war coming? It’s a whole season of people in robes standing in drafty rooms telling each other the war is coming, like Moses on Mount Nebo staring at the Promised Land for forty years and being told he can look but he can’t go in. Critics and fans dinged it for its slow pace, and some reviewers pinned part of it on budget limits.

Now compare the parent show. Original Game of Thrones also spent years promising us an endgame. Winter is coming was the entire marketing department. But on the way to winter, it PAID. Episode nine of season one, the show cut off its leading man’s head, on the steps, in front of his daughters. The season one finale hatched dragons out of a funeral pyre with a naked woman sitting in the ashes. The wait was the main course. The wow was served in courses.

That’s the distinction, and it’s the whole post in one sentence: a long wait is fine. A long wait with no payments is a scam.

The classics knew it.

Breaking Bad, season one, episode two: a body dissolved in acid falls through the ceiling. EPISODE TWO.

Alien: the chestburster arrives at dinner, unannounced, and nobody at that table ever wanted dessert again. Neither did I.

The Sopranos gives you a man strangling a rat with a wire in a college-tour episode in season one, while his daughter is in an admissions interview.

Fringe would hand you a whole cosmology one week and a man melting into goo on an airplane the next.

None of these shows were in a hurry. All of them were patient, slow-cooked, long-game storytelling. And all of them understood the deal: you can make me wait for the endgame as long as you hand me something astonishing at every exit along the highway. Wow factor isn’t a reward at the end. It’s a payment schedule.

The never-ending origin story skips the payments and asks you to trust the balloon payment. And I’ve been around long enough to know what happens to people who trust balloon payments. They end up in Florida.

The Case Against Me, Which Is Annoyingly Good

I’d rather knock down my own argument than let the top comment do it. Here’s the best of what’s against me.

One: House of the Dragon proved the patience pays. Season three premiered June 21, 2026 and opened with the Battle of the Gullet, which the showrunner called “arguably the craziest episode of television ever made.” Critics loved it. The season scored a franchise high on Rotten Tomatoes. So the slow season set up the explosive one. Fair. But look at the calendar. Season one ended on a cliffhanger promising war in October 2022. The war’s first great battle aired in June 2026. That’s season THREE. Three seasons, almost four years, basically a full presidential term spent waiting for a show to start the fight it announced. And “the payoff came eventually” is the exact defense every never-ending origin story will make. Every single one. The question isn’t whether the payment came. It’s whether you made people eat cardboard for four years to get it.

Two: Breaking Bad IS an origin story. Five seasons of a chemistry teacher becoming Heisenberg. Guilty. Except the man was cooking meth in his underwear in the PILOT. The origin story was the spine, not the stall. Every episode moved him a step down the staircase. That’s the difference between a staircase and a waiting room.

Three: the audience disagrees with me. Lanterns got bigger as it went. The finale set the show’s record, and the premiere’s viewership kept climbing over the run toward roughly thirty million. People didn’t bail. They leaned in. Maybe I’m the kid who ate the marshmallow in eleven seconds and is now writing an angry blog about the other kids.

Four: it told a complete story. That’s not nothing in an era of seasons that end on a cliff and a cancellation. Lanterns landed its plane. And an origin story is the honest thing to do when you’re introducing a whole cosmic corner of a new universe to people who have never heard of John Stewart and think Sinestro is a cologne.

Five: Marvel paying off fast didn’t save Marvel. Plenty of people got exhausted by the Disney+ firehose. Fast payoffs and bad payoffs can travel together.

Hold all five. They’re real. Then hold this: nothing on that list requires a show to sit on its biggest toy for eight hours. You can introduce a new universe AND blow something up in episode three. Moon Knight introduced an entire Egyptian pantheon to people who thought Khonshu was a sneeze, and he was in the suit before the first credits.

Cash the Check

Somewhere in a motel room in Rushville, Nebraska, there’s a ring box on a nightstand. You open it. Inside is another ring box. You open that one. Another box. The green glow is right there at the bottom, faint, promising, always one more hinge away.

And down the hall, in a beige room with a one-way mirror, there’s a marshmallow on a plate with a beard down to the floor and a rusted timer next to it, and it’s very, very proud of how long it’s waited.

I’m not asking them to rush. I’m a patient man. I’m a former runner. I know what a long race feels like.

But even a marathon has water stations.

Give me a water station. Give me a head on a step. Give me a body through the ceiling. Give me a bald alien in a space prison and then, for the love of all the Guardians of Oa, let him do something before I’m on Medicare.

Cash the check. Or at least show me there’s money in the account.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Does John Stewart become Green Lantern in the Lanterns finale?

Yes, with an asterisk. In the season one finale, “Dirt and Stars,” which aired October 4, 2026, John Stewart (Aaron Pierre) accepts the role of Earth’s Green Lantern and speaks his own version of the oath. He first gets Hal Jordan’s ring in episode five. Co-creator Tom King said afterward that John is “not a hundred percent a Green Lantern anymore,” and Guy Gardner (Nathan Fillion) still holds the official ring for the sector.

Who created HBO’s Lanterns?

Lanterns was created by Chris Mundy, Damon Lindelof and Tom King. Mundy is the showrunner, Lindelof wrote the pilot, and the series is produced with DC Studios, led by James Gunn and Peter Safran. It premiered August 16, 2026, and ran eight episodes.

When does Sinestro appear in Lanterns?

Sinestro, played by Ulrich Thomsen, debuts at the end of episode two, “Trust Fall,” when Hal Jordan visits him in an intergalactic prison. Showrunner Chris Mundy has said Sinestro was not meant to get his full villainous turn in season one.

What is a never-ending origin story?

It’s a pattern in which a series spends most or all of its runtime building toward the moment a hero takes on their famous identity, then delivers that moment at the very end, sometimes again in the next season. Smallville (ten seasons before Clark flies in the finale) and the Punisher’s repeated resets are common examples.

Is VisionQuest worth watching?

VisionQuest premiered on Disney+ on October 14, 2026, as the third part of the story that began with WandaVision and Agatha All Along. It puts James Spader’s Ultron at the center early, and Jocasta (T’Nia Miller) debuts in episodes three and four. Whether it’s good is your call, but it doesn’t hoard its big characters.

What is the mystery box in TV writing?

The “mystery box” comes from a 2007 TED talk by J.J. Abrams, co-creator of Lost, about a magic box he bought as a kid and never opened because the mystery was more exciting than the contents. It’s become shorthand for shows that run on unanswered questions rather than payoffs.

Rant over. The ring is still in the box. The box is still in the box. And the marshmallow says hi.
