Hegseth Impeachment: What Happens to Maduro and Venezuela?

November 15, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog

# If They Fire the Man Who Stole the King, Does Anybody Have to Give the King Back? (A Venezuelan Falls Down the Hegseth Rabbit Hole Instead of Rewatching Bob’s Burgers)

Let me be upfront about the alternative.

The alternative to this post was Bob’s Burgers. Again. I have watched every season. I know them by heart. I can mouth Linda’s lines a half-second before she sings them, which is a skill, and a cry for help, and a thing my wife has asked me to stop doing in public.

And I’m not knocking it. There’s nothing wrong with spending a Tuesday in a cartoon where the worst thing that can happen is a rival restaurant across the street. I’m just saying when you know a show well enough to dub it, the show stops being a show and becomes a weighted blanket.

So instead I picked the other option. The rabbit hole. The one where you KNOW you’re wasting your time, and you go in anyway, like a Labrador that’s been told the pond is cold.

This is what I found at the bottom. Bring a flashlight. Bring a lawyer. Bring something brown to pour.

The Frat Boy With the Nuclear Clipboard

It started on Sunday.

John Oliver gave a whole episode of Last Week Tonight to Pete Hegseth, the Secretary of Defense, a man the administration also insists on calling the Secretary of War, the way a guy named Kevin insists you call him “The Kev.”

And after watching it, you’re left with one thought that keeps circling the drain: how did anyone appoint this man? Not “why.” Why is easy. Loyalty, television, jawline. I mean HOW. Mechanically. Who signed the paper. Was there a pen. Did the pen try to escape.

We’re talking about a frat boy with a god complex and a nuclear clipboard. The kind of guy who’d do a keg stand at a funeral, then give the eulogy, then ask the widow if she’s seen his sunglasses.

The man once summed up his philosophy of war in a slogan he apparently thought was a T-shirt: “Maximum lethality, not tepid legality.” Read it twice. It rhymes. That’s the only thing it does well. It’s a war crime with a jingle.

Oliver’s team did the polite journalistic thing and asked the Pentagon for comment. According to Oliver, a Pentagon spokesman replied by suggesting they deport John Oliver. Not refute. Not clarify. Deport. The Department of War answered a comedian’s email with a threat of exile, which is the most on-brand thing a government agency has done since the IRS invented the form that asks you to fill out the form.

Hegseth Fact: If a House ever impeaches him, Pete Hegseth will be only the third Cabinet secretary in American history to get the honor. The first was Secretary of War William Belknap in 1876, over a kickback scheme. The second was Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024, over the border. Belknap was acquitted. Mayorkas’s charges were dismissed. The “Secretary of War” title has now been impeached twice, 150 years apart, which is either a coincidence or a curse on the letterhead.

The Voters Showed Up. They Just Brought a Different Crayon.

Here’s the part of Oliver’s episode that rearranged the noggin furniture.

The midterms were November 3. Republicans didn’t lose the House. They lost the House AND the Senate, in a wave that The New York Times estimated at roughly a 13-point loss in the national congressional vote. Democrats flipped districts Trump had carried by as much as 18 points.

And the President’s response, on Truth Social, was to wonder aloud how the polls could have been so wrong. As if the polls had done it. As if a Quinnipiac intern had personally gone door to door.

Here’s what he’s missing, and it’s the whole thing: the Republican electorate DID go out. They did vote. The turnout wasn’t the problem. They just didn’t vote red. They looked at the ballot, looked at the war, looked at their grocery receipt, and picked a different crayon.

Which is why Oliver’s point lands so hard. The people Trump and his cabinet of cronies need to watch now aren’t the Democrats. Democrats were always going to be mad. It’s the Republicans. The ones who want to rebuild the party after this. The ones flabbergasted that it happened on their watch. And the ones staring at the White House doing math in their heads: I lost my job because of these goofballs.

Which brings us to Kentucky.

Enter the Man With Nothing Left to Lose

On Monday, Representative Thomas Massie, a Republican from Kentucky, stood on the House floor and read eight articles of impeachment against Pete Hegseth out loud.

Quick correction to my own rabbit-hole notes, because I’d been calling him a senator in my head all day: Massie is a congressman, House side. And he’s a lame duck. He lost his Republican primary back in May, after spending a year as the guy in the caucus who keeps raising his hand at the wrong moment. He doesn’t need anybody’s endorsement anymore. He doesn’t need a committee seat. He’s cleaning out his desk and taking the stapler with him.

There is no creature more dangerous in Washington than a principled man with nothing left to lose. It’s like a cornered raccoon that has read the Constitution.

He first tried this in September. The Speaker, Mike Johnson, responded by sending the House home early so the vote couldn’t happen, which is the parliamentary equivalent of pretending you didn’t hear the doorbell. The resolution expired. So on Monday Massie filed it again, a 34-page document, and filing it that way forces House leadership to schedule a vote within two legislative days.

When reporters asked what impeachment even is, he gave the cleanest definition I’ve ever heard: “An impeachment is just firing somebody, really.”

Hold onto that sentence. It’s going to come back and bite us. Twice.

Hegseth, for his part, dismissed it as sham articles cooked up by a defeated congressman and his Democrat allies. Johnson said impeaching a defense secretary while the country is at war would look terrible. Which, fair, but the country is at war partly because of the stuff in the articles, so that’s a bit like the arsonist saying now is not a good time to talk about matches.

Here’s the eight, in plain English:

- Articles I, II and III: the Iran war. Started February 28 without Congress. Kept going after the 60-day legal clock ran out. Kept going after both chambers passed a resolution ordering the troops out, the first time in history both houses have ever done that.

- Article IV: ignoring the laws meant to prevent civilian deaths, centered on the U.S. strike on an elementary school in Minab, Iran, on the war’s first morning, which the resolution says killed at least 156 people, most of them girls between seven and twelve.

- Article V: the boat strikes. “Extrajudicial killings.”

- Article VI: retaliating against Senator Mark Kelly for a video telling troops they can refuse illegal orders.

- Article VII: Venezuela. Titled, and I am not making this up, “Kidnapping of a Sovereign Foreign Leader.”

- Article VIII: Yemen.

Eight. Massie’s own line was that the question isn’t whether you like the war, it’s whether the man committed impeachable acts, and the answer is yes, eight times.

I’m Venezuelan. You can guess which one I read first.

Article VII, or: The Night They Took the King Off the Board

Let me paint it the way the articles paint it, because the resolution has more pulp in it than a legal document has any right to. It reads like a Tom Clancy novel if Tom Clancy had a parole officer.

January 3, 2026. An operation code-named ABSOLUTE RESOLVE. More than 200 American special operations troops on the ground in Caracas. More than 150 aircraft and drones: fighters, bombers, armed helicopters, electronic warfare planes. A naval force behind them that included an amphibious ready group and a carrier strike group. They knocked out Venezuela’s air defenses, struck targets around the capital, put an armed team into the city, and took Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, out of the country.

The resolution says at least 83 people were killed, several of them civilians. Seven Americans were casualties.

Then, the same day, the President held a press conference at Mar-a-Lago with Hegseth beside him and said the United States would run Venezuela until a proper transition could happen. And that very large American oil companies would go in, spend billions, fix the broken oil infrastructure and start making money.

That second sentence is the hinge of the whole article. Massie’s argument is that the operation was dressed up as narcotics law enforcement, a drug bust with aircraft carriers, while the actual objective was the oil. His evidence is the President describing the oil plan on the evening of the raid, before the smoke had settled over La Carlota.

And here’s my favorite detail, the one that made me sit up in bed: Article VII says the raid violated Congress’s power to make “Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water.”

That’s real. That’s Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution. The Founders, men in powdered wigs who thought leeches were medicine, sat down and wrote, in ink, that the power to grab things on land belongs to Congress. They wrote a rule about kidnapping before they wrote one about electricity. They saw this coming from the 1780s. They just couldn’t picture the helicopters.

Captures Fact: The “Captures on Land and Water” clause was written mostly with prize ships in mind, the old practice of seizing enemy vessels and cargo at sea. Using it to argue about seizing a head of state is a stretch some scholars will fight about. Massie used it anyway. Two hundred and thirty-some years later, the clause got its first real date.

Double Tap

Before we go further into Venezuela, I have to stop on Article V, because it’s the article that turns my stomach, and because the boats were the opening act of everything that happened in January.

The resolution counts it this way. Starting September 2, 2025, at least 69 separate strikes on boats the administration said were running drugs, 46 in the Eastern Pacific, 21 in the Caribbean. At least 73 vessels hit. At least 243 people killed. No arrests. No trials. No evidence presented in any court. Coast Guard work, done with missiles.

And the first strike, on September 2, a boat carrying 11 people between Venezuela and Trinidad: the strike destroyed and capsized it. Then, about 45 minutes later, with two survivors clinging to the wreckage, a second strike was ordered. It killed them both.

That’s the double tap. You hit the target. You wait. You hit whoever’s still breathing.

I’m not going to make a joke here. Some sentences don’t get a tag. Under the laws of war, a shipwrecked survivor clinging to debris is out of the fight, and you do not shoot them. That’s not a liberal talking point. It’s in the manual. The Pentagon’s own lawyers used to teach it.

The resolution adds a second scene. December 30, three boats hit in the Eastern Pacific, eight people left shipwrecked. Four months into the campaign, nobody had a rescue asset in position. Help arrived around 45 hours later. All eight were dead.

That’s the context Caracas happened in. Not a bolt from the blue. A campaign that had already decided the law was a suggestion.

Tuesday Night, 211 to 204

So here’s how the week ended. Spoiler: not with an impeachment.

Tuesday morning, Hegseth held his first Pentagon press conference in six months, called the whole thing a “political spectacle” and a farce, and called Massie a disgruntled and defeated congressman. Trump said he’d stand by him. “He won two wars.” Which is a sentence that should come with a footnote the size of a phone book.

Tuesday evening, the House didn’t vote on impeachment. It voted on whether to vote on impeachment. Republican leadership moved to send the resolution to the Judiciary Committee, which in House-speak is a nursing home with no visiting hours. The motion passed 211 to 204. Every Republican in the room voted to bury it, except one. Guess who.

Massie had wanted each of the eight articles voted on separately, so members could go on the record one charge at a time. Was the school strike impeachable? The boat strikes? The kidnapping? The referral made sure nobody had to answer any of those questions out loud. A vote designed to avoid a vote. You could hear the sigh of relief from the Capitol rotunda all the way down to Mar-a-Lago.

And here’s the part that should keep a Defense Secretary up at night. Seven votes. That’s the margin. Eleven Democrats didn’t even show up, along with seven Republicans. Earlier that same day, Republicans were privately sweating that weak attendance might accidentally let the thing through. Even GOP critics of Hegseth said they didn’t like him but didn’t think he’d hit “high crimes and misdemeanors,” which is the political equivalent of saying the guy is a terrible driver but you’re not ready to take the keys.

So he survived. He survived a lame-duck House with a Republican majority that had just lost its job, on a procedural motion, by seven votes, with a dozen-plus seats empty.

Now look at the calendar.

On January 3, 2027, the new Congress is sworn in. Democrats hold the House. Democrats hold the Senate. Hakeem Jeffries, expected to be the next Speaker, called himself a “hard yes” before the vote, and his summary of Hegseth to NPR was four words: “He’s got to go.” Chuck Schumer has endorsed the effort. And unlike Massie, a Democratic House doesn’t need a privileged-resolution trick to force a vote. It holds the gavel. It can hold hearings, subpoena the officers who served under him, put them under oath, and build the record that Massie had to assemble from news clippings.

Massie’s resolution itself dies with this Congress. A new one has to be filed. That’s paperwork, not an obstacle.

So this was never the end of the story. It was the dress rehearsal. The question was never really if someone comes back for Pete Hegseth. It’s when, and what happens to everything he did once they get there.

January Fact (the first of two): The new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027. That’s one year to the day after the raid on Caracas. If Democrats want a symbolic date to refile the articles, the calendar already picked one for them.

The Rabbit Hole, With Three Robots

So the night before that vote, instead of Bob’s Burgers, I opened ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude side by side.

This is how I do research now, and I’ll defend it. I don’t trust any one of them. I make them argue. I give one the prosecution, one the defense, and the third gets to be the bailiff who keeps saying “objection, that’s not what the case says.” It’s like hosting a debate between three very well-read interns who’ve never had a hangover.

And I asked them one simple question:

If Pete Hegseth is impeached and convicted, and Congress is basically declaring that what he did was illegal… what happens to the things he did?

Because that’s the question nobody on cable is asking. Everybody was asking whether he’d be impeached this week. Now they’re asking whether he’ll be impeached in January. Nobody’s asking what impeachment would actually UNDO.

We went back and forth for a couple of hours. The robots disagreed on a few things, agreed on most, and I fact-checked the rest against court dockets and news reports like a man checking a used car’s odometer.

What came out is the most troubling thing I’ve read this year. For anybody. And for someone with Venezuelan ties, it’s the kind of troubling that sits on your chest at 3 a.m. like a cat that’s put on weight.

We’re pulling the car over. Hazard lights on. Everything above this line was the story of how we got here, with jokes. Everything below is the answer to the question, and the jokes get thinner, because the answer is a staircase that only goes down.

Here’s How You Remove a Secretary of Defense (And Why It’s Harder Than Removing a President of Venezuela)

First, the mechanics. I’m not a lawyer. I’ve never even played one on TV, and I’ve watched enough Suits to know that’s the only credential that matters.

Impeachment has two stages.

Stage one, the House. A simple majority impeaches. That’s the indictment. The old House just declined to take that step, by seven votes. The new House, the Democratic one, can take it with its own majority starting in January, no Republican help required.

Stage two, the Senate. Conviction takes two-thirds of the senators present. If all 100 show up, that’s 67. If he’s convicted, he’s removed from office immediately, and the Senate can then hold a separate vote, by simple majority, to bar him from ever holding federal office again.

Now do the math. The new Senate has a Democratic majority. A majority. Not 67. To convict, a big chunk of Republican senators would have to cross over, and even Republicans who can’t stand him told reporters this week that “bad secretary” isn’t the same as “high crimes.” So impeachment in a Democratic House looks likely. Conviction in the Senate is a long shot.

But a long shot isn’t a dead one, and the Senate isn’t the only danger. A Democratic House doesn’t need a conviction to make his life miserable. It gets the subpoenas. It gets the hearings. It gets the officers who sat in the room on September 2, under oath, on camera, explaining who ordered the second strike. Every week of that is a week Republican senators have to decide, again, whether they want their names next to his.

So, the realistic range of outcomes runs from “the House impeaches and the Senate shrugs” to “the House impeaches, the Senate holds a long and humiliating trial with a parade of former officers under oath, and he’s removed.” Somewhere in there is a third option, the most Washington one of all: the hearings get ugly enough that he resigns to spend more time with his podcast before anyone has to vote. The walls aren’t closing in yet. But somebody has definitely started measuring the room.

But let’s take the dream scenario. Let’s say he’s convicted. Gone. Barred forever. Congress, on the record, says he did eight impeachable things, one of them titled “Kidnapping of a Sovereign Foreign Leader.”

What happens to the kidnapping?

Does Maduro Walk?

No.

That’s the first answer and it’s the cleanest one. Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores aren’t being held because of the impeachment. They’re being held because of an indictment, a federal narcotics case in the Southern District of New York that goes back to March 2020, Trump’s first term, which was then superseded and unsealed the day they were captured.

Two separate machines, bolted to different floors. Firing the man who drove the van doesn’t unlock the cell.

Here’s where they are right now. Both pleaded not guilty on January 5. Both are in the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn, a building with a reputation that would make a Venezuelan prison guard wince. In October, prosecutors added a new charge, conspiracy to torture political opponents. Their lawyers have filed to dismiss everything on the grounds of head-of-state immunity, and the government’s answer is that the United States hasn’t recognized Maduro as president since 2019, so there’s no head of state to be immune. Oral argument on immunity is scheduled for November 17. One week from today. The trial is set for June 2027.

But wait. If Congress says the raid itself was illegal, doesn’t that poison the whole case? Fruit of the poisonous tree? Every cop show you’ve ever watched?

This is where American law does something that sounds insane until you hear it twice. And then it still sounds insane, just more organized.

It’s called the Ker-Frisbie doctrine. Two old Supreme Court cases, 1886 and 1952, that boil down to: American courts don’t care HOW you got here. If you’re physically standing in the courtroom, the court can try you. Even if you were dragged in.

Then came United States v. Alvarez-Machain, 1992. The DEA wanted a Mexican doctor, Humberto Álvarez-Machain, for his alleged role in the 1985 torture and murder of DEA agent Enrique “Kiki” Camarena. Mexico didn’t hand him over. So in 1990 the DEA paid bounty hunters to kidnap him from his office in Guadalajara and fly him to Texas. Mexico was furious. The case went to the Supreme Court, and in a 6 to 3 decision the Court said the abduction didn’t stop the United States from putting him on trial.

Here’s the kicker, and it’s the kind of detail a Rankin novel would kill for: once he finally got to trial, the judge threw the case out for lack of evidence. He walked. America kidnapped a man across an international border, won the right to try him at the Supreme Court, and then couldn’t prove it. He went home to Guadalajara. There’s no word on whether anybody gave him a ride.

So: even if every senator agreed the raid was illegal, the case against Maduro would not automatically collapse. His lawyers would get new ammunition. They’d wave the impeachment record around like a winning lottery ticket. But the legal default in American courts is that the snatch doesn’t void the trial.

January Fact: Manuel Noriega, the dictator of Panama, surrendered to U.S. forces on January 3, 1990, after the American invasion of Panama. Nicolás Maduro was captured on January 3, 2026. Thirty-six years to the day. Noriega was tried in Miami, convicted in 1992, and his head-of-state immunity argument failed because Washington had never recognized him as Panama’s lawful leader. The prosecution’s playbook for Maduro was written in 1990. They didn’t even change the date.

Who Gives It Back?

Okay. So the man stays in Brooklyn. What about Venezuela? Can Venezuela demand him back?

Sure. Venezuela can demand anything. I can demand a refund from my twenties.

Caracas could take the impeachment record and wave it at every forum on earth: “Look, the United States Congress itself says this was a kidnapping.” It could demand Maduro and Flores be returned. It could demand formal recognition that its sovereignty was violated, compensation, an apology, guarantees it won’t happen again.

And this is where I need to introduce you to the most important footnote in this entire post. Nicaragua.

In 1986 the International Court of Justice, the UN’s highest court, ruled that the United States had broken international law in Nicaragua by mining its harbors and arming the Contras, and that it owed Nicaragua reparations. A real ruling. A real court. A real defeat.

The United States’ response was, roughly, “no.” It had already announced it was walking away from the court’s compulsory jurisdiction. When the UN Security Council tried to call for compliance, the U.S. vetoed it. Nicaragua eventually dropped the claim in 1991 after a friendlier government came to power. The reparations were never paid.

That’s the precedent. Not a theory. A thing that happened. When a court with a robe and a gavel and a building in The Hague told Washington to give something back, Washington checked the box marked “decline” and moved on.

An impeachment conviction wouldn’t even be that. It would be the U.S. Senate saying a U.S. official broke U.S. law. It creates no international obligation to return anybody. To get from “the Senate says it was illegal” to “Maduro gets on a plane to Caracas” you’d need a separate legal process, or a separate diplomatic deal, or a separate miracle.

Which leaves the question that actually keeps me up. The one I asked the robots three different ways to make sure they weren’t humoring me.

What about everything that grew on top of the kidnapping?

The Oil, the Throne and the Fine Print

Let’s do the throne first.

Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro’s vice president, was sworn in as acting president on January 5. Two days after the raid. And the strange truth that the cable panels skip right past is this: the Venezuelan state didn’t fall. The same ruling party is still running the country, minus the man at the top, with Washington leaning over its shoulder.

If the raid was illegal, is her government illegitimate? Not automatically. A government doesn’t stop existing because the way it got there was ugly. Plenty of the governments on the planet today came to power in ways that would not survive a Senate hearing. What an illegality finding WOULD do is hang a permanent question mark over how Venezuela’s political future got decided: by Venezuelans, or by a foreign military and the press conference that followed it.

International law, for what it’s worth, protects a country’s political independence whether or not its government is democratic. That protection doesn’t come with a character reference. It’s not a prize for good behavior. It’s the lock on the front door, and it’s there for the houses with bad tenants too.

Now the oil.

On January 29, 26 days after the raid, Rodríguez signed a new hydrocarbons law that opened Venezuela’s oil industry to private companies and walked back the rule, written by Hugo Chávez in 2006, that the state oil company PDVSA had to be the principal stakeholder in every major project. The new law lets private operators control production and sales and lets disputes go to independent arbitration instead of Venezuela’s own courts. On the very same day, the U.S. Treasury began easing sanctions on Venezuelan oil and widened what American energy companies could do there.

Same day. Pen in Caracas, pen in Washington. A synchronized swimming routine performed in crude.

Then it got more specific. By June, the U.S. had expanded licenses for BP, Chevron, Eni, Maurel & Prom, Repsol and Shell to operate in Venezuela. And the license terms, according to Colombian financial press, said disputes could be settled in the U.S., France, Singapore or the U.K., and that transactions with the Venezuelan government or PDVSA had to be interpreted under U.S. law and U.S. jurisdiction. Companies from Russia, China, Iran, Cuba and North Korea: not invited.

Read that one more time. Deals for Venezuelan oil, governed by American law.

Now. Is American investment in Venezuela illegal? No. Venezuela needs it. Its oil industry is a rusted carcass. More than seven million Venezuelans have left the country since 2014, and a functioning oil sector is one of the few things that could bring some of them home. If someone tells you every barrel that moves is a crime, they’re selling something.

But the argument Massie is making, and the one an impeachment conviction would give teeth to, is narrower and nastier: were these agreements obtained under military coercion? Was a law signed in Caracas less than a month after a foreign army flew out with the president a free choice, or a ransom note with a royalty rate?

If that question ever gets a formal answer, every contract downstream gets a crack in it. Future Venezuelan governments could challenge them. Venezuela could demand compensation. American companies could find themselves defending deals in arbitration with the impeachment record introduced as Exhibit A. We’re talking billions of dollars, and lawyers who bill like they’re paid by the syllable.

And here’s the thing that should make everybody nervous, oil companies included. Venezuela has done this dance before. In the 2000s Chávez tore up the 1990s contracts and nationalized the assets of the companies that wouldn’t go along, including ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips. Those companies are still chasing billions in arbitration awards. Two decades later.

So the pattern is: a government in Caracas grabs the oil, and the companies spend twenty years in court. Now a government in Washington helped grab it back, and if that grab is ever ruled illegal, the next government in Caracas gets to start the clock again.

Oil in Venezuela doesn’t have owners. It has tenants, and a rotating cast of men with guns who think they’re landlords.

Oil Fact: Venezuela sits on the largest proven crude reserves on the planet. It is also, as of this writing, a country where the national oil company has been run into the ground, sanctioned into the dirt, nationalized, privatized and promised to the “biggest anywhere in the world” oil companies at a press conference, all inside of twenty years. The oil has been in the ground for millions of years. It has never once been asked what it wants.

The Risk Problem

Remember Risk? The board game? Little plastic armies, a map of the world, dice that decide fate, and a rulebook folded in the box lid that nobody has read since the Carter administration.

The thing about Risk is that you only play it with people who agree on the rules. The second somebody’s cousin decides he can take Kamchatka from Brazil because “the ocean’s frozen this time of year,” the game is over. Not because he won. Because nobody else is playing anymore.

International law is that rulebook in the box lid. Nobody reads it. Everybody assumes it’s there. It works only as long as the big players pretend to respect it, and the smaller ones believe them.

So here’s the scenario that the three robots and I kept circling back to, and I’m going to state it flat, no metaphor.

Imagine China decides the president of another country is a criminal. Sends in forces. Captures him. Flies him to Beijing for trial. Announces Chinese companies will be “running” that country’s energy sector until a proper transition.

Or Russia does it.

What does Washington say?

Today, the American answer would be: our case was different. We had an indictment since 2020. We never recognized him. He stole an election. He ran a narco-state. Ours was law enforcement with a lot of aircraft.

And some of that is even true, which I’ll get to in a second.

But imagine the U.S. Senate has formally convicted the defense secretary for an article titled “Kidnapping of a Sovereign Foreign Leader.” Now the American answer is: “Well, our own Senate said ours was illegal too, but… do as we say?”

International law can’t be a set of rules that powerful countries pull out of the box lid when convenient and fold back up when it isn’t. Once one player flips the board, everybody else gets to flip it too. And the people who live on the board, the Venezuelans, the Panamanians, whoever’s next, don’t get to be players. They’re the territories.

The Other Side of the Board

Now, the self-rebuttal. Because I promised myself I’d play fair, and because if I don’t write this section, the top comment will.

Here’s the strongest case for what happened on January 3, the way its defenders make it:

Maduro was not a legitimate president. In the 2024 election, the government’s electoral council declared him the winner without publishing precinct-level results. The opposition collected and published tally sheets from the majority of voting machines showing Edmundo González had won. International observers said the vote didn’t meet basic democratic standards. Dozens of governments refused to recognize the result. The U.S. had stopped recognizing Maduro back in 2019. If he wasn’t the lawful head of state, there’s no head of state to kidnap.

He was a wanted man long before any of this. The narco-terrorism indictment dates to March 2020. The government now adds torture conspiracy. The argument goes: arresting an indicted fugitive is law enforcement, even if the arrest required a navy.

The Noriega precedent cuts both ways. Panama 1989 was condemned at the UN as a violation of international law. Noriega was still convicted, and the courts upheld it. The system has looked at almost exactly this situation before and let the prosecution stand.

Venezuelans themselves celebrated. On January 3 there were Venezuelans dancing in the streets of Santiago, Chile, and in diaspora cities across the hemisphere. Political prisoners have been released under an amnesty since. For a lot of families, the raid ended something that elections, sanctions, negotiations and two decades of prayer never did.

And I will say this without a wink, because it’s the part that makes this so hard: none of those points is crazy. Some of them are flat-out true.

The Two Questions That Are Not the Same Question

So here’s where the rabbit hole bottoms out. It comes down to two sentences, and the trick of this whole saga is how hard everyone works to blur them into one.

Sentence one: Nicolás Maduro should answer for the crimes committed under his government.

Sentence two: The United States has the right to bomb Venezuela, take its president, and decide the country’s political future and who pumps its oil.

The first does not prove the second.

Maduro could be guilty of every charge in that New York indictment, every single one, and that still wouldn’t make the operation that captured him lawful. And the reverse is just as true: proving the raid was illegal would not make Maduro innocent. It wouldn’t refund a single tortured prisoner or bring back a single one of the seven million who left.

The legality of the trial in Manhattan and the legality of the raid on Caracas are two separate questions. Only one of them has a courtroom.

And here’s the bit that I can’t get past, the thing all three robots ended up agreeing on even when I tried to bribe them out of it: even in the best case, impeachment only answers a third question. Not “was Maduro guilty.” Not “was the raid lawful under international law.” Just: “did this one man deserve to keep his job.”

There are three different findings that could come out of this, and they are not interchangeable:

1. The Senate convicts Hegseth. That says the Senate thinks his conduct was impeachable. PERIOD. It doesn’t establish that the capture was illegal for every legal purpose.

2. Congress formally establishes that the operation violated American constitutional law. That says the administration overstepped its domestic authority. Better. Still an American finding about American rules.

3. An international court, or some other competent international body, rules that international law was broken. That’s the only one that creates obligations to Venezuela. And see: Nicaragua, 1986, for how that goes.

Massie said it himself, and I keep coming back to it. An impeachment is just firing somebody.

You can fire the guy who flipped the Risk board. You can throw him out of the basement. You can ban him from game night forever.

The pieces stay where they landed.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

What did Thomas Massie charge Pete Hegseth with?

Eight articles of impeachment, filed again on November 9, 2026. Three cover the Iran war and the War Powers Resolution, one covers civilian casualties including the strike on a school in Minab, one covers the boat strikes, one covers retaliation against Senator Mark Kelly, one covers the January 3 capture of Nicolás Maduro (Article VII, “Kidnapping of a Sovereign Foreign Leader”), and one covers strikes in Yemen in 2025.

Was Pete Hegseth impeached?

Not yet. On November 10, 2026, the House voted 211 to 204 to refer Massie’s resolution to the Judiciary Committee, which blocked a floor vote. Massie was the only Republican to vote against the referral.

Could Hegseth still be impeached?

Yes. Democrats take control of the House and Senate on January 3, 2027, and Hakeem Jeffries, expected to be Speaker, has said of Hegseth, “He’s got to go.” Massie’s resolution expires with this Congress, so a new one would have to be filed. A Democratic House can impeach with its own majority, but Senate conviction still needs two-thirds.

Is Thomas Massie a senator?

No. He’s a Republican congressman from Kentucky who lost his primary in May 2026, so he’s leaving Congress in January.

If Hegseth is convicted, is Maduro released?

No. Maduro and Cilia Flores are being prosecuted in a separate federal criminal case in New York. An impeachment conviction doesn’t touch it. Their lawyers could use the record to argue the capture was unlawful, but under the Ker-Frisbie doctrine and United States v. Alvarez-Machain (1992), an illegal abduction doesn’t automatically stop a U.S. trial.

When is Maduro’s trial?

It’s scheduled for June 2027. Arguments on his head-of-state immunity claim are set for November 17, 2026.

Can Venezuela force the U.S. to return Maduro?

Venezuela can demand it, diplomatically and in international forums. But a Senate conviction creates no enforceable international obligation, and the United States has ignored international rulings before, most famously the ICJ’s 1986 Nicaragua judgment.

Does an illegal raid make Delcy Rodríguez’s government illegitimate?

Not automatically. It would raise serious questions about how Venezuela’s political arrangement came about, but it doesn’t erase the current government or restore Maduro.

Could Venezuela’s new oil deals be challenged?

Possibly, over time. The argument would be that agreements made under military pressure weren’t freely made. American investment itself isn’t illegal. The fight would be about coercion, and it could take decades, the same way Exxon and ConocoPhillips are still pursuing awards from Chávez’s 2000s nationalizations.

How many votes does it take to remove Hegseth?

A simple majority in the House to impeach, and two-thirds of senators present to convict, which is 67 if all 100 vote.

Has the U.S. done this before?

Yes. Panama, 1989: the U.S. invaded, Manuel Noriega surrendered on January 3, 1990, and he was convicted in Miami in 1992. The UN General Assembly condemned the invasion. The conviction stood.

Back to the Basement

So that was my Monday night. Three robots, two hundred browser tabs, one constitutional clause about captures on land. Then Tuesday came, and the House put the whole thing in a drawer by seven votes.

And honestly? It didn’t change the answer one bit. The drawer gets opened in January. Maybe February. The pieces are already on the board, the new players are already pulling up chairs, and the man with the clipboard just found out how few votes stand between him and a hearing room. It’s coming, or something like it is.

Which is why I keep landing in the same place: the most consequential thing about this impeachment isn’t whether it passes.

It’s what it can’t do.

It can’t open a cell in Brooklyn. It can’t tear up a law signed in Caracas 26 days after the helicopters left. It can’t put the rulebook back in the box lid once the whole planet has watched someone use it as a coaster. The most it can do, the very most, is put a name on the record next to the word “illegal” and let history do the slow work that courts and senates won’t.

Which, as a Venezuelan, is not nothing. We’ve spent twenty-plus years being told nobody would ever be held accountable for anything. Accountability that arrives late and partial and in English is still a thing we know how to recognize.

But it’s not giving anything back.

Massie called it just firing somebody. Turns out the House couldn’t even manage that this week. But January is coming, and even when they finally do, it’ll be what he said. A firing. Not a refund.

I’m going back to Bob’s Burgers now. At least in that world, when somebody breaks something, there’s a scene where they have to make it right, apologize, and then everybody eats.

Burger of the Day, chalked on the board in my head: The Absolute Re-Swollen Burger. Comes with a side of oil you can’t send back.
