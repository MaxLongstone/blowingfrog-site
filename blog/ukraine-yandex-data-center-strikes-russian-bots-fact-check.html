Did Ukraine Bomb Russia’s Bots? Tin Hat vs. What’s Real

October 17, 2026  ·  Blowing Frog
Part of The Attention Machine  ·  Chapter 4 of 5 →

The Week Ukraine Unplugged Russia’s Google, the Whole Internet Saw Ghosts, and I Got Punked by a Newspaper From a Town With No People In It

Domino, or How My Brain Learned to Bluescreen

Back in 2005 I watched Keira Knightley play a bounty hunter in Domino.

Not an actress playing a bounty hunter. A fashion model who QUIT modeling to become a bounty hunter. Which is a true story. Domino Harvey, daughter of the actor Laurence Harvey, Ford model, then a woman who kicked in doors for bail bondsmen in Los Angeles. Tony Scott directed it like a man who’d drunk the color grade.

My brain could not process it. It did the thing an old Mac does when you ask it to open Photoshop and a browser at the same time: the little beach ball of death, spinning, spinning, the fans screaming, the RAM sitting in a corner with a cold compress on its forehead asking for a minute.

I genuinely thought that was the oddest sentence the universe would ever hand me.

Sweet summer child. That record has been broken so many times it’s now a pile of vinyl crumbs on the floor of history, and this past week the universe broke it again, set the crumbs on fire, and posted the video.

Here’s the sentence: Ukraine bombed Russia’s Google, and Americans started arguing about whether it rigged the US midterms.

Beach ball. Spinning. Go get a cold compress, you lightweight. I’ll wait.

What Actually Blew Up

Let’s do the facts first, clean, because everything after this gets slippery and I want you standing on something solid when the floor goes.

Between October 8 and October 11, 2026, Ukrainian drones hit three data centers belonging to Yandex, the company everyone calls “Russia’s Google” because it does search, maps, taxis, food delivery, payments, cloud hosting and, lately, artificial intelligence. The sites were in Sasovo (Ryazan region), Kaluga, and Vladimir, east of Moscow.

Sasovo caught fire and shut down. That one housed two of Yandex’s three supercomputers, which have the best names in the whole story: Lyapunov and Chervonenkis. They sound like a pair of disgraced Victorian magicians. They train Russia’s AI models. Trained, past tense, for the moment.

Vladimir went dark too. Yandex said so itself on Telegram.

Then, on Wednesday, October 14, drones hit a data center at Balakovo owned by Sberbank, Russia’s biggest bank, which Reuters described as the largest data center in the country.

Kyiv’s stated reason: payback. In September, Russia struck data centers in the Kyiv region and knocked out internet for roughly 100,000 households for a while. Moscow said those sites served Ukrainian military intelligence. Ukraine’s position, more or less, is “we always respond in kind,” which is the foreign-policy equivalent of a kid saying “he started it” while holding a flamethrower.

And the fallout was real, and weird, and spread far past Russia. Yandex services stuttered across the country. Yandex Go, the taxi-and-dinner app, glitched for users in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Armenia and Belarus. Somewhere in Bishkek a man stood on a curb holding his phone up to the sky like a shaman, waiting for a car that was never coming, because a drone in Vladimir had eaten his ride.

Data Center Fact After the first strike, Yandex reportedly blurred all four of its own cloud data centers on Yandex Maps. Russia’s Google erased itself from Russia’s Google Maps. Russia’s digital development minister told the Duma his office is working with the Defense Ministry on protecting the sites, some lawmakers want them moved underground, and a source told Kommersant replacement equipment could take up to six months to arrive. Six months of buffering. Somewhere a Russian teenager is watching the loading wheel and aging.

“A Little Easier to Breathe”

Now the part where it went sideways.

Cloudflare, which sees a staggering slice of the world’s web traffic, publishes charts of automated requests by network. After the strikes, the line for Yandex’s network did a swan dive. Euronews reported automated traffic from Yandex down about 94 percent compared with the second half of September.

A chart like that is catnip. People screenshotted it, slapped it next to footage of a burning server hall, and the internet arrived, collectively, at a conclusion: the Russian bots are dead.

Then, on October 12 at the Warsaw Security Forum, Volodymyr Zelensky poured gasoline on it. He said that when Ukraine’s strikes reached Yandex, it became “a little easier to breathe on many social media platforms.” Less hate. Less division. Fewer provocations. Fewer bots. And, he added, it was pretty clear where those bots had been coming from.

He also got a jab in at Washington, telling the room it was clear that Putin does not read Truth Social. Which, as diplomatic burns go, is a 7 out of 10 with a twist of lemon.

And off went the memes.

Users in the US swore the robo-replies under conservative posts had gone quiet. Users in Germany said the same about posts backing the AfD, users in the UK about Reform. Somebody christened the whole thing “Operation Quiet Piggy,” which is not a Ukrainian military codename. It’s a meme name. Futurism ran a piece on Elon Musk’s engagement on X looking shriveled the same week, like a grape left on a radiator. Occupy Democrats announced that Zelensky “might just have saved our democracy,” which is a lot of weight to hang on a search crawler.

And the jokes landed where you’d expect: nobody on Truth Social was ever real. It was all bots. The bots lived in Russia. The bots were bought. The bots, the theory went, were Republican turnout in a box, and Zelensky had just curb-stomped the GOP’s midterm campaign with a drone that cost less than a Honda Civic.

Beautiful. Satisfying. A whole story with a villain, a hero, and an explosion.

Hold that thought. Hold it tight. You’re going to want something to throw in a minute.

STOP.

Pull the car over. Engine off. Hazards on. I have to confess something, and I’d rather do it now than have you find it in the comments from a guy named Dale.

I Got Punked by a Newspaper From a Town With No People In It

When I sketched this post, the headline in my head was: Trump and his people accused Zelensky of interfering in the US elections. Called it an act of war against America.

I believed it. I said it out loud, into my phone, with conviction. I had my outrage pre-heated to 400.

It never happened.

The story traces back to The Betoota Advocate, an Australian satire site that bills itself as the country’s oldest newspaper and is named after Betoota, a real outback ghost town with no permanent residents. Its piece, published October 14, had Trump aboard Air Force One saying Ukraine had “gone too far this time.” It spread across the internet stripped of anything that said JOKE on it. Snopes rated it “Originated as Satire.” Lead Stories found no matching reports from any established outlet. Trump did speak to reporters that week, in Bullhead City, Arizona, and didn’t mention Ukraine, Russia or Yandex.

There was a second one, too, from an American satire Substack: the White House accusing Ukraine of “interfering with Russia’s election interference” and lamenting that the strikes had silenced millions of concerned American patriots named Dmitri. That one I’d have caught. Probably. Maybe. After coffee.

And I did not find an “act of war” statement from anyone in the administration. If you have one, send it, and I’ll eat this paragraph with a fork.

Now, sit with what just happened, because it’s the whole post.

The story I wanted to write was about bots flooding the zone with fake political content. And the thing that flooded MY zone with fake political content was... a joke, written by humans, shared by humans, believed by a human. Me. I’m the human. No robot required. I did the bots’ job with my own thumbs and didn’t even get the ruble.

The bots got the week off. We covered their shift.

Unpaid. Like interns who also bring the bagels.

What Washington Actually Said (It’s Bad Enough)

Here’s the part that isn’t satire, and you’ll notice it doesn’t need any help.

The real accusation came from Jack Posobiec, the MAGA commentator, who posted on X that “Zelenskyy is attempting to sabotage Trump in the midterms now” and called for cutting off intelligence sharing and funding. That’s a commentator with a big following. Not the White House. The difference matters, and the internet ate it anyway.

Meanwhile, the actual Trump-Zelensky relationship was cratering for entirely un-bot-related reasons:

  • Trump announced a deal with Putin to sell Russian diesel onto world markets to bring down fuel prices before the midterms. Zelensky blasted it.
  • Trump told CNN Zelensky “could’ve settled this war many times,” and called for Ukraine to hold elections. Ukrainian law bans elections under martial law.
  • On Newsmax, Trump said he’s not a big fan of Zelensky and called Ukraine’s strikes on Russian oil refineries a disgrace.
  • Elon Musk said Zelensky went “too far” with the refinery strikes.
  • And Ukraine’s Starlink connection got throttled for about 24 hours, hampering its drone defenses. Reuters reported the order came from the White House. Trump said, “it wasn’t us,” and that he didn’t think it was Elon either.

So the real fight is about oil, diesel, gas prices and a US election, and Ukraine keeps hitting the things that make Russia money. Data centers are just the newest thing on the list. You don’t need a bot conspiracy to explain why Washington is irritated. You need a gas pump.

Meanwhile, in Buenos Aires

Down here the Milei government never threw a fit. Not on the record, anyway. I went looking, in two languages, and came back with nothing but crumbs.

It didn’t matter. The memes threw the fit for them.

Argentine timelines filled up with the official meltdown that never was: libertarian outrage, cabinet tantrums, the full opera, built like a South Park episode that slipped its leash, wandered into traffic, then into a ministry, then into a press conference, where it grabbed the microphone and refused to give it back. And the star of the best ones is a newscaster avatar straight out of the Kent Brockman school of broadcasting... the helmet hair, the baritone of doom, the eyebrows of a man who has personally witnessed the end of civilization twice before lunch, or came home and found his wife in bed with his oldest friend (it’s a coin toss), gravely informing the nation that “The bots are down... And Milei is out of friends.”

Delivered with a straight face. Which is the whole trick.

Same disease as the Betoota story, different hemisphere. The joke put on a suit and sat behind a news desk, and the suit did all the convincing. Nobody checks the credentials of a man with that much hairspray.

And the reason the joke lands so easily here is that Argentina already has its own bot comedy, homegrown, no drones required.

Back in August, a post by Santiago Oría, the presidency’s director of audiovisual production, drew a pile of replies praising the government that, per reporting from LatAm Journalism Review and El Destape, came with their programming showing: replies that opened with labels like “Reply1:” and “Reply7:”. The robots left the price tags on. Like a guy at a wedding with the size sticker still running down the leg of his rented suit.

Nobody needed to bomb anything to find those. Somebody just forgot to delete a colon.

Two columns. Laminate it, put it on the fridge, quiz your uncle at Thanksgiving.

Look at the left column. Every single item is a story that felt like a fact. Look at the right column. Half of it is boring. Crawlers. Walk-backs. Satellite throttling. That’s the tell. Reality almost never has a villain who twirls a mustache on cue. Reality has a guy in a server room reading a Cloudflare dashboard with a cold tea.

The Drawer Marked “Possible”

Now for the stuff that is neither proven nor nonsense. The junk drawer. The one with the takeout menus, a dead AA battery and a key to nothing.

Could some influence operations have been running on Yandex Cloud? Sure, possible. Yandex Cloud rents servers like any cloud company does, and bot operators rent servers. But researchers quoted by the CBC say it’s unlikely Russian propaganda networks depended entirely on those three buildings. These outfits are built to survive takedowns. One Tom’s Hardware piece noted Yandex’s own systems began auto-migrating workloads to surviving sites. Bots are cockroaches with a VPN.

Could the “quiet” people felt online have been real? Here’s my favorite theory of the week, from a German sociology student named Jan Birkmann posting on Threads: maybe believing the bots were gone made humans engage MORE with each other and scroll past the junk, so the vibe really did improve, powered by nothing but faith. The placebo internet. A sugar pill for your timeline. Honestly? I’d take it.

Did the historian Timothy Snyder have a point when he posted that a lot of pro-Trump and pro-AfD bot work runs on Russian data centers? It’s a serious man making a plausible claim. It is also, as of today, unproven, and the chart everyone waved at doesn’t prove it. Possible drawer. Not the fact shelf.

Is this the new shape of war? This one moves closest to the real column. The Atlantic Council argues Russia’s “sovereign internet” laws herded its digital life into a handful of mega data centers, which turned them into big fat targets. Lithuania’s prime minister reportedly floated asking Ukraine to hit more Russian data centers as a way to protect allied elections. Data centers are becoming what oil refineries were last year: the soft belly. Whoever owns the compute now owns a target. I’m not freaking Jack Ryan, I can’t tell you the drone model, but I can tell you a war that targets search engines is a new kind of war.

The Part Where I Argue With Myself

Let me argue the other side, hard, so the top comment doesn’t have to.

Zelensky’s claim was propaganda too. He’s a wartime president with every reason to sell a win. “Social media got nicer when we bombed Russia” is a fantastic line in Warsaw and a weak claim on a chart, and his own office softened it within a day. If you spent this week dunking on MAGA for falling for bot theories, you were also, in all likelihood, falling for a bot theory. Just the flattering one.

These are civilian facilities. Yandex runs taxis and grocery apps. A woman in Almaty couldn’t order dinner because of a drone in Vladimir. Russia justified hitting Ukrainian data centers by claiming military intelligence used them; Ukraine is answering in kind, and the AI supercomputers at Sasovo arguably make that a strategic target rather than a symbolic one. I’m not an international lawyer and I’m not going to pretend the line is clean. Reasonable people are going to land in different places, and the people who live near the next target are going to land hardest.

And the right’s anger isn’t crazy just because the Betoota story was fake. A foreign government taking action that a big chunk of Americans believe was aimed at their side, a month before an election, is going to make people furious whether or not the bots were real. Perception is a political fact, even when it isn’t a factual one.

Both things are true. Zelensky oversold it. The internet overbought it. Everybody went home with a receipt for something that didn’t exist.

So Who Won the Midterms in a Warehouse in Ryazan?

Nobody. Not yet. Not there.

The real lesson isn’t about Russia’s servers. It’s about mine. The one between my ears. The one that, faced with a satire site from an Australian town of zero people, spun up its little beach ball, skipped the fact-check, and hit SEND on its own outrage.

Back to Domino for a second. The supermodel bounty hunter, the story that froze my brain solid in 2005? That one was true. And the President calling a drone strike in Russia an attack on American democracy, which slid into my head without so much as a speed bump? Fake. The same month the government decided what to call a piece of software, I couldn’t tell a newspaper from a joke.

That’s the new world, kid. The absurd thing is real. The plausible thing is a joke. The bots are probably still out there, humming in a basement somewhere, unbombed and unbothered, watching us do their work for free.

Let the RAM cool. Check the source. Then panic, if you still want to. At least you’ll be panicking about something that happened.

Quick Questions From the Cheap Seats

Did Ukraine bomb Russian data centers?

Yes. Ukrainian drones struck three Yandex data centers in Sasovo, Kaluga and Vladimir between October 8 and 11, 2026, and a Sberbank data center at Balakovo on October 14. Yandex confirmed damage and service outages.

Did Trump accuse Ukraine of election interference over the strikes?

No. That story came from The Betoota Advocate, an Australian satire site, and fact-checkers including Snopes and Lead Stories traced it there. The commentator Jack Posobiec did accuse Zelensky of trying to sabotage Trump in the midterms, but he is not a government official.

Did the strikes take out Russian bots on social media?

There’s no evidence they did. Cloudflare data showed a large drop in automated traffic from Yandex’s network, but researchers say that traffic was mostly search-engine crawlers. Bot-tracking groups reported no measurable change in known pro-Kremlin accounts, and Zelensky’s office softened his claim the day after he made it.

What is “Operation Quiet Piggy”?

A meme name social media users gave the theory that pro-Trump accounts went silent after the strikes. It is not a Ukrainian military operation.

What is Yandex?

Russia’s largest tech company, often called “Russia’s Google.” It runs search, maps, ride-hailing, food delivery, payments, cloud services and AI development, and its services are used across Russia and parts of Central Asia.

Why did Ukraine target data centers?

Ukraine says it was retaliation for Russian strikes on Ukrainian data centers in September. Analysts also point to the strategic value: the Sasovo site housed two of Yandex’s AI supercomputers, and Russia’s internet is concentrated in a small number of very large facilities.

Will this affect the 2026 US midterms?

Nobody can show that it will. US officials have said they expect Russia to use disinformation around the November 3 election, but there’s no evidence these specific strikes changed what Americans see online.
